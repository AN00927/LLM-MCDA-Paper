\begin{abstract}
Everyday decisions like adjusting a thermostat or scheduling an appliance can lower household carbon footprints, but evaluating trade-offs across cost, emissions, comfort, and convenience requires multi-criteria expertise most residents lack. Households fall back on fixed habits or rules of thumb that forgo potential savings. Large language models (LLMs) make interaction easy, yet unassisted models struggle with physical calculations and numerical scoring. We benchmark three general strategies for integrating an LLM into multi-criteria decision support: direct scoring, example-guided scoring, and hybrid reference scoring, tested across 195 household energy scenarios. Extracting scenario parameters for a deterministic reference calculator reproduces the ground-truth ranking more reliably than having the LLM score alternatives directly, which performs little better than chance. The approach extends to other domains with an existing deterministic calculator, pairing it with LLM parameter extraction instead of having the model compute the result.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec1}
The average U.S.\ household emits roughly 8{,}744 pounds of CO\textsubscript{2}e and consumes about 10{,}791 kWh of electricity each year \cite{epa_household2024,eia_faq_electricity2024}. Globally, residential energy consumption has grown at roughly 1\% per year since 2000, a trend driven by increasing household sizes and comfort demands in emerging economies \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}. However, a considerable portion of this usage is adjustable, creating opportunities to reduce both household spending and environmental impact \cite{dietz2009,ohler2020}.

Using Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) \cite{huang2011}, households can identify those opportunities without specialized training. Within a correctly specified value model, it produces a ranking consistent with the decision-maker's stated preferences across all criteria simultaneously. However, constructing that model (defining alternatives, criteria, weights, value functions, and normalization rules) requires expertise and time that routine household decisions cannot absorb.

Large language models carry broad knowledge about residential energy use and product characteristics, and prior work has begun using them to automate the construction or scoring step in an MCDA workflow \cite{svoboda2024,icaart26}. We identify three ways a researcher can place an LLM inside that workflow: scoring alternatives directly (direct scoring), scoring them with retrieved worked examples as guidance (example-guided scoring), or extracting engineering parameters for a deterministic reference calculator to score (hybrid reference scoring). These strategies carry different engineering costs beyond their expected ranking accuracy: direct scoring needs no supporting infrastructure, example-guided scoring requires a maintained retrieval index of worked examples, and hybrid reference scoring requires a maintained deterministic calculator, so the right choice depends on how much accuracy that infrastructure buys. No prior study has compared these strategies against a deterministic reference calibrated to established engineering standards for household energy decisions.

We pose three research questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1}: What architectures are available for integrating an LLM into an MCDA workflow?
\item \textbf{RQ2}: Which of those architectures most accurately reproduces a deterministic Multi-Attribute Value Theory (MAVT) reference model's ranking for household energy decisions?
\item \textbf{RQ3}: How consistently does that ranking hold across LLMs of varying capability and provider?
\end{itemize}
We answer these by benchmarking all three architectures against a physics-based MAVT ground truth across 195 household energy scenarios spanning three decision types (HVAC setpoints, appliance scheduling, and shower duration), using identical MAVT weighting and model access across multiple LLMs, so that the integration strategy is the variable under test.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit-review}
This section reviews prior work on household energy decision-making and adoption barriers, LLM numeric reliability, and LLM integration with MCDA methods.

\subsection{Household Energy Decision-Making and Adoption Barriers}

Globally, residential buildings account for 18\% of CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}; in the United States, the residential sector consumed 19.2 quadrillion BTU (5,627 TWh) of delivered energy in 2025 and accounted for roughly 20\% of national energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{eia2022}. Dietz et al.\ (2009) estimated that household behavioral changes could reduce U.S.\ emissions by 123 MtC (approximately 451 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e) annually \cite{dietz2009}. At the time, this would account for roughly 8.3\% of U.S.\ energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions. Scaling to the U.S.'s 4{,}904 MMmt of 2025 energy-related emissions \cite{eia_co2_2025} suggests a current savings potential of 407 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e. HVAC systems alone account for roughly a third of global residential energy consumption \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}.

\citet{attari2010} found that consumers undervalue energy use and savings by a factor of 2.8 on average, systematically underestimating high-energy activities while overestimating low-energy ones; the ``usual setting'' for most activities rests on incorrect or arbitrary assumptions, whether this is a temperature setpoint, an appliance run time, or shower time. Even a streamlined MCDA calculator \cite{huang2011,huangburgherr2024} still requires framing alternatives, criteria, and weights, keeping the tool out of routine household use.

In households, space heating accounts for 43\% of residential energy use, followed by appliances (13\%), water heating (12\%), and air conditioning (8\%) \cite{eia_recs2020}. These four categories are tested in this study as three decision types: HVAC, appliance scheduling, and water heating via shower duration. Behavioral plasticity, how likely people are to implement each action given financial and social incentives, is captured in \citet{dietz2009}'s Reasonably Achievable Emission Reduction (RAER) metric and determines which reductions are practically attainable.

\subsection{LLM Numeric Reliability and MCDA Integration}

For arithmetic, LLMs often provide incorrect answers unless self-checked against multiple generated solution paths \cite{imani2023mathprompter}. Scoring closely competing alternatives is therefore unreliable without external grounding. There are a few methods to resolve this: few-shot prompting places examples similar to the target problem in the prompt, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) injects external facts into an LLM's context to reduce hallucination in open-domain question answering (QA) \cite{lewis2020}. An MCDA scoring task is closer to Case-Based Reasoning, which treats problem-solving as retrieving similar prior cases and reusing their recorded solutions \cite{aamodtplaza1994}. \citet{minorkaucher2024} found that retrieved example explanations used as in-context demonstrations improve LLM-generated outputs over zero-shot prompting.

\citet{zheng2023judging} formalized the broader LLM-as-judge paradigm, in which a model's own generated scores substitute for human ratings. \citet{wang2024fairescalers} show that LLMs exhibit positional bias when scoring, such as ranking the first response higher because of its placement, and have inaccurate score calibration. \citet{li2026scoringbias} document systematic scoring-bias distortions in LLM-as-a-judge pipelines that persist even in advanced models.

An MCDA problem specifies a finite set of alternatives evaluated against multiple criteria, each carrying a weight that reflects its relative importance to the decision-maker. The formalization originates in classical decision theory \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. The classical toolkit spans several method families beyond MAVT: the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) derives weights from pairwise comparisons, the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) ranks alternatives by distance from an ideal and an anti-ideal point, and outranking methods such as ELECTRE and PROMETHEE compare alternatives pairwise without collapsing them into a single index \cite{huang2011}. 

MAVT aggregates single-attribute value functions into a unified score using criterion weights. However, the used importance weights often fail to reflect effective trade-offs unless the scores are scaled by realized attribute ranges within a choice set \cite{vonnitzsch1993}. Some inaccuracy is integrated into 'fuzzy' methods, such as fuzzy AHP and fuzzy TOPSIS represent decision-maker judgments as fuzzy numbers rather than crisp weights \cite{zadeh1965}, but a human still supplies the judgment. Recent studies use LLMs to automate preference elicitation, alternative generation, or direct scoring \cite{svoboda2024,icaart26}.

\subsection{MAVT Framework \& Weighting Design}
\label{sec:mavt-design}
MAVT compares alternatives on different criteria based on the range of values that occurs for each criterion \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. We hold the weights constant across all decision types, all architectures, and the reference calculator. The MAVT additive form (Eq.~\ref{eq:mavt_sum}) requires preferential independence among criteria \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mavt_sum}
s_j = \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i \cdot v_i(x_{ij})
\end{equation}

We weight criteria as follows: Environmental Impact (35\%), Energy Cost (30\%), Comfort (20\%), and Practicality (15\%) (Table~\ref{tab:item3}). Results are also reported for equal weights and for objective weight vectors derived by entropy and by MEREC (Method based on the Removal Effects of Criteria) \cite{merec2021} in ~\ref{app:weights}.

The Environmental Impact weight of 0.35 reflects the share of national CO\textsubscript{2} emissions attributable to the residential sector \cite{dietz2009} and Value--belief--norm theory's finding that environmental orientation is the most consistent normative driver across pro-environmental household behaviours \cite{stern1999}. Entropy-based normalization of the scenario distributions independently gives high weight to this criterion \cite{roszkowska2026} (\ref{app:weights}). Energy Cost is weighted at 0.30 because cost is the strongest predictor of consumer adoption \cite{newell2014,attari2010,harrispoll2024}. %todo: this energy cost line is too small

In our dataset the energy cost and environmental impact criteria are not statistically independent: Spearman's $\rho$ between them across the 195 test scenarios is 0.92. Preferential independence describes preference structure, regardless of how correlated the attribute values are in a given alternative set \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. The correlation does raise a redundancy, or double-counting, concern, which arises if the two criteria are operationally the same quantity under different names, causing their combined 65\% weight to be applied twice to a single underlying attribute \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}. The extent to which this occurs differs by decision type. Because ranking is performed within a scenario, the relevant quantity is the within-scenario association between the two criteria across the three alternatives.

For the Appliance decision type the two criteria separate. The price period is set by the utility's time-of-use window (for PECO, 2--6\,p.m.\ on weekdays \cite{peco2021}) while the emissions period is set by the PJM system-wide peak of 7\,a.m.--11\,p.m., so a cycle run at 8\,a.m.\ is off-peak for cost and on-peak for emissions. The magnitudes differ as much as the windows. For HVAC and Shower they do not separate meaningfully. In both, the parameters that distinguish the emissions or water quantity from the cost quantity (the occupancy-resolved emissions factor for HVAC, the flow rate and heater setpoint for Shower) are scenario-level and therefore identical across the three alternatives. Within any one scenario the environmental quantity is then a fixed scalar multiple of the cost quantity. For HVAC and Shower types the four-criterion specification therefore operates as a three-criterion one, with an effective weight of 0.65 on a single cost/emissions dimension. This is not universal; the two would diverge on a grid whose marginal emissions intensity varies independently of retail price, which the PJM footprint studied here does not (Section~\ref{sec:calculator-simplifications}).

Comfort is weighted at 0.20, reflecting its role as the second driver of HVAC behavior \cite{aqilah2022} via ASHRAE\,55 setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020}. For appliances, it follows demand-response acceptance research on tolerance for scheduling delay \cite{paetz2012,waseem2020}, and for showers it peaks near the REU2016 average duration and penalizes heater setpoints below CDC Legionella thresholds \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026}. Practicality, weighted at 0.15, captures the feasibility and sustainability of an alternative, which is seperate from likelihood of a one time choice. Low adoption is a longer term effect of friction \cite{ariely2001}. A 20-minute shower in a four-person household with a 50-gallon tank illustrates this: at 2.5\,GPM the four showers together draw about 155 gallons of hot water against the roughly 40 gallons the tank can usefully supply, so the household runs out. The comfort criterion wouldn't account for this, which is why a separate practicality component is necessary.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:norm_x}
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for increasing criteria}\\[4mm]
\dfrac{x_{\max} - x}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for decreasing criteria}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Energy cost and environmental impact enter as raw physical quantities (dollars, and lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2} or gallons of water); comfort and practicality are emitted by the calculators already on a 0--1 utility scale, so for those two $x$ is that score rather than a physical measurement. Each criterion's raw quantity is first converted to a direction-adjusted normalized value $\tilde{x}$, so that higher values are always better:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linear_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \tilde{x}
\end{equation}

The environmental and energy cost functions use the linear value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:linear_vf}): equal absolute changes in these quantities represent equal improvements \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:log_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \frac{\ln\!\left(1 + \alpha \cdot \tilde{x}\right)}{\ln(1 + \alpha)}
\end{equation}

The comfort and practicality criteria use the logarithmic value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:log_vf}) because both exhibit diminishing marginal returns: initial improvements from an uncomfortable or impractical baseline yield disproportionately large perceived gains, while incremental improvements near the optimum matter progressively less. We set $\alpha = 1.5$ for comfort and $\alpha = 1.2$ for practicality a priori to introduce mild concavity, adjusting them only to avoid values leaving the 0--1 range. The values are not elicited or fitted. Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity} reports the architecture ordering across the $\alpha$ sweep, which does not change.

  \end{cases}

\end{equation}
\noindent where $P(u)$ is a unitless multiplicative penalty on the (post value-function) energy-cost score, $u$ is monthly budget utilization, $C_{\mathrm{monthly}}$ is estimated monthly energy/water-related cost for the decision type, and $B$ is the household-stated monthly budget for utilities.

  Q_{\mathrm{int}}  &= 400\,n + A_f + 800, &
  Q_{\mathrm{sol}}  &= 20\,(0.15\,A_f),
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hvac_load}
\end{equation}

  \begin{cases}
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} + Q_{\mathrm{int}} + Q_{\mathrm{sol}} & \text{cooling} \\[3pt]
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} - Q_{\mathrm{int}}                    & \text{heating}
  \end{cases}\right)
  \label{eq:hvac_load_total}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{Q}{1000\,\mathrm{EER}}\,H\,r(o),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{EER} = 1.12\,S - 0.02\,S^{2}
  \label{eq:hvac_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $R$ is the envelope R-value, $A_f$ the conditioned floor area (ft\textsuperscript{2}), $m_h$ the housing-type envelope multiplier (1.2 apartment and condo, 1.5 townhouse and rowhouse, 1.7 single-family), $\Delta T$ the indoor--outdoor temperature difference, $h_c$ the ceiling height, $\mathrm{ACH}=0.35$ the infiltration air-change rate, $n$ the household size, $H=8$ the decision-period length in hours, $S$ the rated SEER, and $r(o)$ the occupancy multiplier. Solar gain enters only in cooling season, and internal gain reverses sign in heating season because occupant and equipment heat offsets the load the system must supply. The 800\,BTU/hr standby term keeps a zero-$\Delta T$ setpoint from producing exactly zero load. Environmental impact applies an occupancy-resolved PJM marginal emissions factor to that energy use, using the peak/off-peak split of Eq.~\ref{eq:emissions}. Age- and maintenance-driven efficiency degradation is not applied to this energy path; it appears instead as a reliability factor on the practicality score.

Comfort uses a tent function anchored to ASHRAE\,55 PMV-optimal setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020} ($76\,^\circ$F in cooling mode, $70\,^\circ$F in heating mode), with an additional penalty term for household sizes $n > 3$:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{comfort}} = \tfrac{1}{10}\max\!\Bigl(0,\; 10 - \lvert T_{\mathrm{in}} - T^{*}\rvert - \tfrac{(n-3)\,0.3\,\lvert T_{\mathrm{in}} - T^{*}\rvert}{3}\Bigr)
  \label{eq:hvac_comfort}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $C_{\mathrm{comfort}}$ is a 0--1 comfort score, $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the indoor setpoint, and $T^{*}$ is the context-dependent optimal setpoint used as the peak of the tent function.

Practicality utility follows a piecewise value function of setpoint extremity, indoor--outdoor $\Delta T$, and equipment age. The extremity slopes steepen in the least-tolerable directions:
\begin{equation}
  \delta(T_{\mathrm{in}}) =
  \begin{cases}
    1.0\,(71 - T_{\mathrm{in}}) & \text{cooling, } T_{\mathrm{in}} < 71 \\[3pt]
    1.5\,(T_{\mathrm{in}} - 82) & \text{cooling, } T_{\mathrm{in}} > 82 \\[3pt]
    1.8\,(63 - T_{\mathrm{in}}) & \text{heating, } T_{\mathrm{in}} < 63 \\[3pt]
    0.8\,(T_{\mathrm{in}} - 76) & \text{heating, } T_{\mathrm{in}} > 76 \\[3pt]
    0                             & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:hvac_practicality}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\delta(T_{\mathrm{in}})$ is the extremity penalty (score points per \textdegree{}F beyond adoption-comfort bounds); the pre-feasibility score is $\mathrm{clip}_{[0.5,\,10]}(10-\delta(T_{\mathrm{in}}))$, multiplied by an outdoor--indoor $\Delta T$ feasibility factor and by $(1-D)$, where
\begin{equation}
  D = \min\!\Bigl(0.30,\; \delta_{\mathrm{maint}}\bigl[\,1.5\min(a,10) + 0.5\max(a-10,0)\,\bigr]\Bigr)
  \label{eq:hvac_degradation}
\end{equation}
\noindent is the fraction of efficiency lost to age and maintenance (capped at 30\%), $a$ is HVAC age in years, and $\delta_{\mathrm{maint}}$ is the base annual loss rate.

An ``Off'' alternative (no active conditioning) is scored with zero decision-period energy, cost, and emissions; its comfort and practicality are instead evaluated against the free-float indoor temperature the building drifts to with the system off. These are outdoor $+5$\,\textdegree{}F in cooling season and $+10$\,\textdegree{}F in heating season, a modeling assumption reflecting solar and internal gains that hold a closed, occupied house above outdoor air temperature, not a value tabulated by \cite{ashrae55_2020,accamanualj}.

Energy cost per cycle is kWh/cycle multiplied by the location- and time-specific TOU rate, resolved to one of six Pennsylvania utilities (PECO, PPL, West Penn, Penelec, MetEd, Duquesne) \cite{peco2021,ppl2025,firstenergy2025,firstenergy2026,duquesne2026} by mapping household location to a serving utility and then that utility's peak/off-peak window. The PJM emissions window (7am--11pm) applies to environmental impact regardless of the utility's billing window, because emissions follow the regional grid. The calculator does not model weekend, holiday, or seasonal peak-window shifts, using weekday summer TOU rates year-round.

Comfort utility is parameterized by delay, appliance type, housing type, household size, and run-time hour (late-night runs, 22:00--07:00, incur a noise penalty); practicality utility by delay, run-time hour, and household coordination difficulty. Delay is the minimum circular distance from the baseline time $t_0$ on a 24-hour clock:
\begin{equation}
  d(t,t_0)=\min\!\bigl((h(t)-h(t_0))\bmod 24,\; (h(t_0)-h(t))\bmod 24\bigr),
  \label{eq:appliance_delay_circular}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $d(t,t_0)$ is delay in hours between run time $t$ and baseline time $t_0$ and $h(\cdot)$ maps a time to hour-of-day. The base comfort component is a piecewise decay from 10 at zero delay, with appliance-specific tolerance ceilings of 12\,hr (dishwashers), 8\,hr (washers), and 6\,hr (dryers). Demand-response acceptance research \cite{paetz2012,waseem2020} establishes that such ceilings exist, not where they fall:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{base}}(d)=
  \begin{cases}
    10                                  & d=0 \\[3pt]
    10-\frac{2}{3}\,d                   & 0<d\le 3 \\[3pt]
    8-\frac{1}{2}\,(d-3)                & 3<d\le 7 \\[3pt]
    6-\frac{2}{5}\,(d-7)                & 7<d\le 12 \\[3pt]
    2                                   & d>12
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_base_comfort}
\end{equation}

The late-night noise penalty scales with housing type: apartment and condo units receive the largest multiplier ($\kappa=1.5$) because shared walls transmit vibrations directly, townhouse and rowhouse an intermediate ($\kappa=1.2$), and isolated single-family homes the smallest ($\kappa=0.8$), and it is joined by a household-size penalty; \citet{waseem2020} supports the direction of that effect, not its magnitude. The penalty fires when the appliance's rated sound level exceeds 45\,dBA, which penalizes washers and dryers (74 and 65\,dBA) and leaves a dishwasher, rated at 45\,dBA, exactly at the threshold and therefore unpenalized. Timing complexity adds a practicality penalty at run-time hours whose low-price zones sit at the brink of the day, perceived as too early or too late to act on \cite{paetz2012}.
\begin{equation}
  N(t,\mathrm{type},\mathrm{housing})=
  \begin{cases}
    0 & \text{if } h(t)\in[7,22) \text{ or } \mathrm{dBA}(\mathrm{type})\le \theta \\[3pt]
    2\cdot \kappa(\mathrm{housing}) & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_noise_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $N(t,\mathrm{type},\mathrm{housing})$ is the noise-disruption penalty in comfort-score points, $\mathrm{dBA}(\mathrm{type})$ is the appliance noise level, and $\theta = 45$\,dBA is the threshold. The household-size penalty scales with delay relative to the appliance-specific maximum acceptable delay $d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})$:
\begin{equation}
  S(d,n,\mathrm{type})=\sigma(n)\cdot \min\!\left(\frac{d}{d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})},\,1\right),
  \label{eq:appliance_size_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\sigma(n)$ steps from 0.0 for fewer than 3 occupants, to 0.8 for 3--4, to 1.5 for 5 or more. Final comfort is $C_{\mathrm{comfort}}=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,10]}(C_{\mathrm{base}}-N-S)$.

For budget utilization in \eqref{eq:budget_penalty}, the appliance calculator converts per-cycle cost to a monthly estimate using appliance-specific cycles-per-month estimates, from 215, 300, and 283 cycles/year for dishwashers, washers, and dryers respectively \cite{energystar_dishwasher_criteria2023,energystar_clotheswasher,doe_dryer_tp_2011}.

Practicality mirrors the delay schedule with additional penalties for late-night timing complexity and household coordination difficulty. The base practicality component is:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{base}}(d)=
  \begin{cases}
    10                                  & d=0 \\[3pt]
    10-d                                & 0<d\le 2 \\[3pt]
    8-\frac{3}{4}\,(d-2)                & 2<d\le 4 \\[3pt]
    6.5-\frac{1}{2}\,(d-4)              & 4<d\le 8 \\[3pt]
    4.5-\frac{3}{8}\,(d-8)              & 8<d\le 12 \\[3pt]
    1.5                                 & d>12
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_base_practicality}
\end{equation}
\noindent Timing complexity applies an additive penalty by run-time hour:
\begin{equation}
  T(t)=
  \begin{cases}
    2 & 0\le h(t)<6 \\[3pt]
    1 & 22\le h(t)<24 \\[3pt]
    0 & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_timing_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent and household coordination difficulty uses the same delay scaling form as in comfort:
\begin{equation}
  K(d,n,\mathrm{type})=\kappa_n(n)\cdot \min\!\left(\frac{d}{d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})},\,1\right),
  \label{eq:appliance_coordination_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\kappa_n(n)$ is an occupants-based factor increasing with occupants. Final practicality is $C_{\mathrm{prac}}=\mathrm{clip}_{[1.5,10]}(P_{\mathrm{base}}-T-K)$, with a daytime floor applied for $h(t)\in[7,22)$ and $d\le d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})$.

Mains inlet temperature is derived from outdoor temperature between 45\,\textdegree{}F (winter) and 65\,\textdegree{}F (summer) \cite{burch2007}, setting the fraction of flow requiring heating to reach a $105\,^\circ$F delivery target \cite{zhang2023}; shower energy and environmental impact (water volume) follow from that mixing fraction, flow rate, and duration, and energy cost applies a flat electricity rate to that energy:
\begin{equation}
  f = \min\!\left(1,\;\max\!\left(0,\;\frac{T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}{T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)\right),
  \qquad
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{f\,G\,\rho\,(T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}})\,t}{3412\,\eta}
  \label{eq:shower_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $f$ is the hot-water mixing fraction, $T_{\mathrm{d}} = 105\,^\circ$F the delivery target, $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ the mains inlet temperature, $T_{\mathrm{tank}}$ the water-heater setpoint, $G$ the flow rate in GPM, $\rho = 8.33$\,lbs/gallon, $t$ the duration in minutes, 3412 the BTU-per-kWh conversion, and $\eta = 0.92$ the electric water-heater efficiency. Because $f$ and the temperature rise $T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$ move inversely, their product reduces to $T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$, which is why shower energy depends on the inlet temperature but not on the heater setpoint for any setpoint at or above the delivery target.

Comfort depends on duration, heater setpoint, household contention, and outdoor temperature, with a fixed penalty outside the 120--140\,\textdegree{}F heater band for mixing margin and Legionella amplification risk below it and standby loss or scald risk above it \cite{cdc2026}. The comfort-optimal duration follows the REU2016 7.8-minute average above 77\,\textdegree{}F and reaches about 10.3 minutes at the winter cap (10\% per 10.8\,\textdegree{}F drop). This cold-weather elasticity is a modeling assumption supported only in direction by the literature \cite{reu2016}, rather than a directly reported coefficient, and the cap is a cross-population extrapolation from a southern-Spain sample [todo: WHILE SKIMMING, this sentence seemed very ai. please fix, and other similar sentences in this section.]; warm-weather scoring is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{comfort}}(\tau,T_{\mathrm{heater}},n)
  = \mathrm{clip}_{[0,10]}\!\Bigl(C_{\mathrm{base}}(\tau)
  -\Delta_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_{\mathrm{heater}})
  -\Delta_{\mathrm{cont}}(\tau,n)\Bigr).
  \label{eq:shower_comfort_full}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\tau$ is shower duration (min), $n$ is occupants, $C_{\mathrm{base}}(\tau)$ is the base comfort curve, $\Delta_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_{\mathrm{heater}})$ is a penalty, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{cont}}(\tau,n)$ is a household-contention penalty.

Practicality, on the other hand, depends on duration and tank-capacity feasibility, where the hot water required is the per-shower draw multiplied by the number of occupants, so household size enters practicality through the capacity test rather than as a separate term:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{need}} = n\,\tau\,\dot V\,f_{\mathrm{hot}}, \qquad
  H_{\mathrm{avail}} = 0.8\,S.
  \label{eq:shower_capacity_need_avail}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $H_{\mathrm{need}}$ is total hot-water volume required for the household's showers over the relevant period, $H_{\mathrm{avail}}$ is available hot-water draw volume, and $S$ is nominal tank capacity (all in gallons); a fixed penalty is applied when $H_{\mathrm{need}}>H_{\mathrm{avail}}$.

For budget utilization in the budget penalty equation, shower monthly cost is estimated from per-shower cost using an average showers-per-person-per-day rate of 0.9 \cite{reu2016}. The anchor points that set the band boundaries are sourced from the 7.8-minute REU2016 average, the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold, the CDC Legionella range, and the ASHRAE\,55 comfort band \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026,ashrae55_2020}.

The penalty magnitudes and slopes between those boundaries are design choices for which the literature fixes a direction but not a size. Storage-tank studies report usable capacity ratios of 70--85\% of nominal volume across inlet configurations \cite{abdidin2024}, and the DOE first-hour rating procedure ends a draw once outlet temperature falls 15\,\textdegree{}F below its maximum \cite{shen2021}, which places the 0.80 usable-tank fraction inside a measured range without pinning it there. The same holds for the discrete capacity-exhaustion penalty \cite{marini2017}, the steepening of the duration curve past 15 minutes \cite{wilkes2005}, and the 0.5 per-minute contention penalty at four or more occupants \cite{rouleau2019}: each has a supported sign and an unsupported magnitude.

The shower practicality curve measures likelihood of adoption, so it is not centered on the 7.8-minute comfort optimum. Beyond the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold the curve declines conservatively: 33\% of adults self-report showers longer than 15\,min \cite{harrispoll2024}, while the metered REU2016 data implies a much lower true rate \cite{reu2016}.

\subsection{Architecture Design and Robustness Testing}

The three conditions are direct scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$), example-guided scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$), and hybrid reference scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$). Across all three we hold constant the criterion weights, the scenario set, the temperature, and the underlying model within each run.

\label{sec:architectures}
All three architectures call the same model per test via OpenRouter at temperature 0.3, with model held constant within each run. The study evaluates four models: GPT-OSS-20B (reasoning, low effort), Qwen 3.5 9B (non-reasoning), DeepSeek V4 Flash 0423 (hybrid reasoning), and Gemini 3.5 Flash (reasoning, minimal effort), each set to its lowest available reasoning tier (OpenRouter model identifiers and routing variant in Supplementary Material S1). We fixed alternative order in the prompt to scenario-sheet order across all shipped runs; Section~\ref{sec:alternative-order} reports the test of whether that choice matters. Ties in the MAVT aggregate were broken deterministically by the priority order environmental, energy cost, comfort, practicality (full tie-rate breakdown by model and architecture in Supplementary Material S1).

The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ architecture (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}) issues one API call per alternative (three calls per scenario) using a system prompt that combines role-prompting, structured-output constraints \cite{icaart26}, and bias-mitigation language from the NIST AI bias framework \cite{nistsp1270}. Beyond the scenario description, the prompt supplies no external context. 
The system prompt establishes the analyst role and, for each of the three decision types, gives each of the four criteria a short anchor phrase describing what a good and a poor score look like on that criterion. It closes with an output-format instruction and a reminder to distinguish between criteria (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1).
The user prompt provides the scenario context and instructs the model to score one alternative at a time. 

The anchors are qualitative. For HVAC and Shower, each criterion is described by a good/moderate/poor triple stated in terms of the physical situation. The Appliance anchors reference the off-peak and peak rate periods and the overnight minimum of the grid-emissions profile, coarser domain information than the HVAC and Shower anchors carry. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s prompts essentially  leave Pennsylvania-specific quantities to the model: given the location, it must supply the PECO time-of-use tiers and the PJM marginal emissions factors from its own knowledge.

The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ architecture retrieves the single most similar scenario ($k=1$) from a ChromaDB vector store before scoring, using the sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model. The retrieval count $k=1$ was selected from the development-set ablation in Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation-methods}. The retrieval index is built once from the 90 RAG-only scenarios described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}; test scenarios are used only as query vectors \cite{lewis2020}. Like $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ uses three API calls per scenario, one per alternative. The retrieved exemplar is rendered as a full worked example: its household-reported parameters, its engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle, tank size, heater setpoint, etc.), and each of its alternatives with the four reference criterion scores plus the MAVT aggregate and rank. The model is instructed to use the exemplar as reference but to score the target scenario independently.

The system prompt keeps $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s analyst role, output format, and criteria-distinction instruction, but heavily simplifies the anchors: lower cost, lower emissions, higher comfort, easier implementation. The retrieved exemplar supplies scoring guidance in the anchors' place (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1). The user prompt presents the target scenario, the alternative to score, and the retrieved exemplar.


The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ architecture issues a single API call per scenario for structured parameter extraction. Known household-reported scenario fields (outdoor temperature, square footage, insulation tier, household size, housing type, utility budget, and location) are passed directly to the calculator without LLM involvement, and the three alternatives are taken verbatim from the scenario sheet. The LLM is asked only to estimate the withheld engineering parameters. For HVAC, this is the wall R-value, SEER rating, HVAC age, and occupancy rating, with the occupancy context available from the question text. For Appliance, the kWh/cycle and current baseline time, which is also inferrable from the question text. For Shower, the numeric GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint.

We check extracted parameters against physical bounds for each quantity (bounds in Supplementary Material S1). An out-of-range or non-numeric value counts as a failed extraction; we merge validated parameters with the known fields and pass them to the decision type's reference calculator.

The extraction prompt uses decomposed prompting: it does not instruct the LLM to score alternatives, only to extract the withheld engineering parameters, with an instruction to estimate rather than omit any mandatory field. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ invokes the same calculators that generated the reference scores. \subsubsection{Prompt ablation}
\label{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}
The prompt ablation tests whether the reported ordering reflects the design of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ or the particular prompts used to implement them. This suite runs on the same 195 test scenarios. There are three abalations: removing the anchors that define the endpoints of the scoring scale (unanchored variant), inserting a chain-of-thought scaffold that instructs the model to reason before emitting its scores (reasoning-scaffold variant), and rescaling the response range from 0--1 to 0--10 with scores divided by ten before scoring (rescaled-response variant). $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ ships without anchors, so the unanchored variant applies to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ alone. This is why the results matrix has 28 architecture--model--variant cells, not 32. IT is not run on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ because none of the three manipulations has a counterpart in its extraction prompt. Cells run five times per model, or three for Gemini. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ control and unanchored cells were extended to ten runs for DeepSeek, GPT-OSS and Qwen because the five-run $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$/Qwen unanchored comparison fell close to $\alpha = 0.05$ (Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation}).

Point estimates use the per-scenario aggregation defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}: each run produces one Kendall's $\tau$ and one Top-1 value per scenario; these are averaged across runs to give one value per variant, architecture, model and scenario, and significance is tested across the 195 scenarios within each architecture--model stratum.

\subsubsection{RAG ablation}
\label{sec:rag-ablation-methods}
The RAG ablation runs on the complete 90-scenario RAG dataset under a leave-one-out protocol: each scenario serves as the query with itself excluded from retrieval. Seven configurations are compared against the $k=3$ control of the time: retrieval $k$ at 1 and at 5, an embedding swap replacing all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with the smaller paraphrase-MiniLM-L3-v2, exemplars with the hidden engineering parameters removed, exemplar descriptions with the reference scores and ranks removed, random dataset draws in place of similarity-ranked exemplars, and an offline \texttt{nearest-neighbor} configuration that assigns the retrieved scenario's scores directly with no LLM call.

Two of the seven configurations are controls: random exemplars for retrieval quality, and \texttt{nearest-neighbor} for whether unmodified exemplar copying carries ranking signal on its own.

\subsubsection{Parameter-provenance ablation}
\label{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}
The third suite isolates how much of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy comes from the LLM's parameter extraction step, as opposed to a deterministic calculator scoring the alternatives on its own (full results in Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}). It also runs on the 195 test scenarios. \texttt{extracted} supplies the values $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s LLM returned for each scenario. \texttt{default\_params} supplies one fixed value per hidden parameter, the dataset median for numeric parameters and the dataset mode for categorical ones, reused for every scenario with no per-scenario inference, making it the floor a calculator-only system would reach if extraction contributed nothing beyond a constant guess.

The withheld parameters match those described for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ in Section~\ref{sec:architectures}: R-value, SEER, HVAC age, and occupancy context for HVAC; appliance type, kWh/cycle, and baseline time for Appliance; GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint for Shower. A scenario whose extraction failed is excluded from the \texttt{extracted} condition's metrics, following the sentinel-exclusion convention (Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}).

\subsubsection{Alternative ordering}

Each call names the alternative under evaluation, states the scenario context, and lists the remaining two alternatives on a single line for context. Since every model call is independent without context on how the other alternatives were scored [todo: add for A_E and A_D] specifically not for A_H, the only influential reversal is changing order of how the context is introduced. %todo: what does that last clause mean? refine.  

In every condition, reversing the order means reversing the three alternative values as they appear in the scenario record, so that the value listed first becomes the value listed last. For $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ this surfaces only in the context line described above. We rescored all 195 test scenarios in reversed order for each of the four models and compared each reversed run against that model's five shipped runs on top-1 correctness (per-model shift and significance in Section~\ref{sec:res-order}). Because the ranking tie-break resolves exact ties by input order, reversal can flip a fully tied choice set on its own, which biases the comparison toward detecting an effect and makes the observed null conservative.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction call passes the whole scenario record, including all three alternatives, so reversal changes the order in which the model reads them. Any effect would be indirect, acting through the estimated physical parameters that feed the calculator, which produces the ranking. We therefore re-ran $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction over all 195 test scenarios in reversed order, five runs per model. In every condition we passed the extracted parameters to the calculator with the alternatives restored to canonical order, so the ranking tie-break cannot manufacture a difference between conditions. We test whether the reversed runs are distinguishable from the shipped-order runs by an exact permutation test that reassigns the reversed label across all $\binom{13}{5} = 1287$ ways of choosing five of the 13 runs. \subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}
Accuracy is evaluated on scoring and ranking. Score error uses Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) between architecture scores and reference scores on the 0--1 MAVT scale, computed separately for each criterion then aggregated across criteria. Ranking accuracy uses Kendall's $\tau$ between the architecture's induced ranking of the three alternatives and Top-1 accuracy (whether the architecture's best-ranked alternative matches the reference ranking). Each per-scenario $\tau$ is averaged across the 195 scenarios by arithmetic mean. With $n=3$ alternatives, per-scenario $\tau$ can take only discrete values ($-1, -\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, 1$) before averaging. A uniformly random ranking gives Top-1 accuracy of $1/3$ and expected Kendall's $\tau$ of $0$. Efficiency is evaluated using total tokens (input plus output) and cost, both summed across the 195-scenario test set. Scenarios that fail are not included in calculation. We separately compute a run-to-run standard deviation, treating runs rather than scenarios as the unit of analysis.

An alternative failure-handling rule, imputation, is reported in Supplementary Material S2: scenarios that fail receive the 0.5 scale midpoint for the affected criterion scores before that run's metrics are computed.

The paired Wilcoxon comparisons treat the scenario as the unit, computing each of the 195 scenarios' metrics from the mean over the runs in which that scenario was scored successfully, which is five for most scenarios and fewer where a run failed. The paired bootstrap confidence intervals resample scenarios while carrying all successful runs of a drawn scenario together. The paired Wilcoxon tests on the main architecture comparison are Holm-corrected across a single 56-test family; the 24 comparisons on the three reported primary metrics ($\tau$, MAE, and Top-1) remain significant at the 0.05 level (full test-family enumeration in Supplementary Material S2). Models are not pooled


Failure is recorded per scenario via a reserved sentinel value (1928). \subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
\label{sec:comparison}

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ qualifies prior claims that internal consistency implies correct output: \citet{svoboda2024} showed GPT-4 can construct an AHP model within consistency-ratio thresholds and \citet{icaart26} found structured output improves ranking consistency in COMET-based MCDA, yet in our case $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, across multiple runs, had low-variance scores while still being far from correct (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}). This miscalibration generalizes the scoring biases documented by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024fairescalers} and Li et al.~\cite{li2026scoringbias} from judge contexts to multi-criteria decision scoring. Where that literature predicts compressed scores, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s within-scenario range runs 1.25--2.00 times the reference range (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}). This study benchmarks that miscalibration against a deterministic reference calculator instead of other LLM judges.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ extends Case-Based Reasoning \cite{aamodtplaza1994,minorkaucher2024} to MCDA scoring, but its gain over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ plateaus because exemplar scores anchor the output scale without correcting the model's scenario-specific arithmetic (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}). $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ sidesteps the numeracy problem \cite{imani2023mathprompter}, rather than improving LLM arithmetic through chain-of-thought prompting \cite{wei2022cot} or tool augmentation, with a single extraction call and an existing calculator. The closest prior example, Huang and Burgherr's streamlined MCDA calculator~\cite{huangburgherr2024}, still requires users to possess MCDA expertise. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ requires that expertise from whoever designs and audits the domain calculator rather than from the household.

\subsection{Limitations of the Study}
\label{sec:limitations}

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is graded against the same calculators it uses. It shares the reference model's deterministic calculator but withholds several inputs, estimating them from homeowner-accessible text; the physical validation bounds (R-value, SEER, GPM ranges) derive from ASHRAE and ENERGY STAR literature rather than from pilot LLM outputs. A misspecified formula would inflate $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ while potentially hurting the other two architectures. An independent audit with a separately built calculator would help solve this (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

\label{sec:calculator-simplifications}
There are calculator formulas true relationship is unpublished or cannot be evaluated on the scenario data: mains water temperature interpolated linearly from outdoor temperature \cite{burch2007}, monthly appliance cycle rates, a fixed shower tank-availability factor of 0.8, and a run-time-fraction approximation for HVAC occupancy. These affect all architectures equally, but the absolute MAE and RMSE values reflect the combined error of LLM extraction and calculator imputation. Results are also specific to Pennsylvania's grid and utility rate structure: the PJM marginal emissions factors and Pennsylvania time-of-use rate tiers used throughout this study do not generalize to high-renewables grids, where lower and more variable emissions intensities would compress environmental score distributions, and absolute MAE values for the environmental and energy-cost criteria would shift. Additionally, all shower scenarios assume electric resistance water heating; gas, heat-pump, and tankless configurations lie outside the current scope.

The scenario dataset was generated by crossing categorical parameters with representative numeric draws, which ensures parameter-space coverage but does not reflect real-world joint distributions: older houses tend to have worse insulation and lower SEER ratings together, and the synthetic dataset decouples that. Accuracy in real-world use may therefore be lower.

Each scenario presents three specific alternatives a household might compare; real decisions involve a continuous, unstructured space. With $n=3$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s correlated scenario-level errors cancel more often than they would in a larger choice set. Per-scenario Kendall's $\tau$ can take only the four values $-1$, $-1/3$, $+1/3$ and $+1$, so every per-scenario $\tau$ reported in this paper is a mean over a four-level variable, and the reported $\tau$ of 0.899 is an upper bound on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy for larger choice sets. Generalizing to nearby alternatives rests on a monotonicity assumption that holds for cost and energy but not always for comfort. Additionally, we assume a single decision-maker with fixed weights. 

 None of the three architectures produces a calibrated confidence estimate alongside its ranked recommendation, and a correct ranking and an inverted one arrive in the same form, with no signal that lets a homeowner tell them apart. Any deployment should expose the estimated parameters and the per-criterion scores alongside the ranking, so the household has something to check. %todo: this isnt a true limitation. while the confidence part canbe briefly discussed, its not smth for here, maybe furuter work.

A homeowner who weighs comfort above its 20\% weight, tolerates a colder shower than ASHRAE~55 predicts, or works a shift that rules out a daytime wash would need an adjusted calculator. \subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}
The most immediate follow-up is an external review of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction schema and validation bounds. A complementary check would have a household-decision expert score a sample of scenarios blind to the calculator's output, validating the reference ranking against human judgment.

Cross-grid replication under different emissions--cost correlation structures (e.g., ERCOT's real-time pricing or a high-nuclear European grid where marginal emissions approach zero during surplus) would address the PJM specificity noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. A domain-transfer test would apply $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ to a mature calculator outside energy (e.g., actuarial life-expectancy or portfolio Sharpe-ratio estimation). An adversarial stress test would violate the calculators' structural assumptions to identify where $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage degrades.

Longer-term extensions include multi-turn preference elicitation from multiple household members and a randomized controlled trial comparing LLM-MCDA recommendations against unaided decisions in deployed households.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We benchmarked three strategies for integrating an LLM into an MCDA workflow against a deterministic MAVT reference across 195 household energy scenarios. The ordering $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ holds for every model tested, and the metrics measure fidelity to the specified reference model rather than real-world decision accuracy.

The contribution is an evaluation framework and an open reference implementation for LLM-fronted MCDA in residential energy. It supplies three deterministic MAVT calculators, a 285-scenario dataset split into 195 test and 90 retrieval cases, and a harness that reports Kendall's $\tau$, Top-1, MAE and RMSE per model and per decision type. All three are public, so the comparison can be rerun under a different grid, weight vector, or model without rebuilding the reference.

A language model belongs in a pipeline that already has, or could have, a deterministic way to check its answer. Asking a model to read a situation and produce a judgment in one step requires it both to understand the situation and to compute the outcome, and the judgment inherits whatever scoring biases and arithmetic weaknesses the model carries into that second task. Asking the model only to translate an unstructured description into the inputs a validated calculator can score removes that second task. The model doing the translating no longer has to be the strongest one available: which of the four models supplied that translation step barely moved the result, while the choice of model moved the result by a wide margin whenever a model scored the alternatives itself. Run-to-run agreement was not, on its own, a signal of which architecture to trust: models agreed with themselves across runs while nearly all answers were wrong. This should generalize to any setting where a language model is asked to produce a number a domain-specific calculator could compute instead.

That restructuring is only available where a vetted calculator exists. Where none is available, a non-LLM fixed default outranks the retrieval-augmented direct-scoring design on overall Kendall's $\tau$ for all four models, at zero LLM cost. That design beats the fixed default only on Appliance decisions, where a static schedule cannot track a household's actual timing constraints. Its case rests on the specific decision types where per-scenario reasoning matters, not overall Kendall's $\tau$.
