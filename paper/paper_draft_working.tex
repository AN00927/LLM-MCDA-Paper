\begin{abstract}
Everyday decisions like adjusting a thermostat or scheduling an appliance can lower household carbon footprints, but evaluating trade-offs across cost, emissions, comfort, and convenience requires multi-criteria expertise most residents lack. Households typically fall back on fixed habits or rules of thumb that leave savings on the table. Large language models (LLMs) make interaction easy, yet unassisted models struggle with physical calculations and numerical scoring. We benchmark three general strategies for integrating an LLM into multi-criteria decision support: direct scoring, example-guided scoring, and hybrid reference scoring, tested across 195 household energy scenarios. Extracting scenario parameters for a deterministic reference calculator reproduces the ground-truth ranking more reliably than having the LLM score alternatives directly, which performs little better than chance. The approach generalizes beyond household energy: wherever a deterministic calculator already exists, pairing it with LLM parameter extraction avoids asking the model to compute the answer itself.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec1}
The average U.S.\ household emits roughly 8{,}744 pounds of CO\textsubscript{2}e and consumes about 10{,}791 kWh of electricity each year \cite{epa_household2024,eia_faq_electricity2024}. Globally, residential energy consumption has grown at roughly 1\% per year since 2000, driven by increasing household sizes and comfort demands in emerging economies \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}. However, a considerable portion of this usage is adjustable, creating opportunities to reduce both household spending and environmental impact \cite{dietz2009,ohler2020}.

Tools that combine optimization, knowledge of human boundaries, and decision support could recognize those opportunities, but only if people can use them without specialized training. One such framework is Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) \cite{huang2011}. Within a correctly specified value model, it produces a ranking consistent with the decision-maker's stated preferences across all criteria simultaneously. However, constructing that model (defining alternatives, criteria, weights, value functions, and normalization rules) requires expertise and time that routine household decisions cannot absorb.

Large language models carry broad knowledge about residential energy use and product characteristics, and prior work has begun using them to automate the construction or scoring step in an MCDA workflow \cite{svoboda2024,icaart26}. We identify three ways an LLM can be placed inside that workflow: scoring alternatives directly (direct scoring), scoring them with retrieved worked examples as guidance (example-guided scoring), or extracting engineering parameters for a deterministic reference calculator to score (hybrid reference scoring). These strategies carry different engineering costs beyond their expected ranking accuracy: direct scoring needs no supporting infrastructure, example-guided scoring requires a maintained retrieval index of worked examples, and hybrid reference scoring requires a maintained deterministic calculator, so the right choice depends on how much accuracy that infrastructure buys. No prior study has compared these strategies against a deterministic reference calibrated to established engineering standards for household energy decisions.

We pose three research questions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1}: What architectures are available for integrating an LLM into an MCDA workflow?
\item \textbf{RQ2}: Which of those architectures most accurately reproduces a deterministic Multi-Attribute Value Theory (MAVT) reference model's ranking for household energy decisions?
\item \textbf{RQ3}: How consistently does that ranking hold across LLMs of varying capability and provider?
\end{itemize}
We answer these by benchmarking all three architectures against a physics-based MAVT ground truth across 195 household energy scenarios spanning three decision types (HVAC setpoints, appliance scheduling, and shower duration), using identical MAVT weighting and model access across multiple LLMs so that the integration strategy, not the underlying model or weighting scheme, is the variable under test.

Section~\ref{sec:lit-review} reviews household energy decision-making barriers, evidence on LLM numeric reliability, and prior LLM-MCDA integration work. Section~\ref{sec2} covers the MAVT reference framework, the three architectures under comparison, and the evaluation metrics; Section~\ref{sec:dataset} details the scenario dataset and its deterministic reference calculators. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports ranking accuracy, extraction error propagation, and robustness results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets those findings against prior work and states the study's limitations before Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit-review}

This review synthesizes three lines of prior work bearing on the present study: barriers that keep households from optimizing energy use unaided, evidence on how reliably LLMs score and reason numerically without external grounding, and the nascent, largely unbenchmarked literature on pairing LLMs with MCDA.

\subsection{Household Energy Decision-Making and Adoption Barriers}

Globally, residential buildings account for 18\% of CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}; in the United States, the residential sector consumed 19.2 quadrillion BTU (5,627 TWh) of delivered energy in 2025 and accounted for roughly 20\% of national energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{eia2022}. Dietz et al.\ (2009) estimated that household behavioral changes could reduce U.S.\ emissions by 123 MtC (approximately 451 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e) annually \cite{dietz2009}. At the time, this would account for roughly 8.3\% of U.S.\ energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions. Scaling to the U.S.'s 4{,}904 MMmt of 2025 energy-related emissions \cite{eia_co2_2025} suggests a current savings potential of 407 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e. That potential concentrates in a few areas: HVAC systems alone account for roughly a third of global residential energy consumption \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}.

\citet{attari2010} found that consumers undervalue energy use and savings by a factor of 2.8 on average, systematically underestimating high-energy activities while overestimating low-energy ones; the ``usual setting'' for most activities rests on incorrect or arbitrary assumptions, whether this is a temperature setpoint, an appliance run time, or shower time. Constructing MCDAs is time-consuming even for experts \cite{huang2011}; even a streamlined calculator \cite{huangburgherr2024} still requires framing alternatives, criteria, and weights, keeping the tool out of routine household use.

In households, space heating accounts for 43\% of residential energy use, followed by appliances (13\%), water heating (12\%), and air conditioning (8\%) \cite{eia_recs2020}. These four categories are tested in this study as three decision types (HVAC, appliance scheduling, and water heating via shower duration), and account for the large majority of controllable residential energy use (not accounting for purchases). Behavioral plasticity, which is how likely people are to implement each action based on financial and social incentives, captured in \citet{dietz2009}'s Reasonably Achievable Emission Reduction (RAER) metric, determines which reductions are practically attainable. \citet{attari2010} found that individuals often point to curtailment rather than installing new products, even though equipment-based actions score higher with behavioural adoption in \citet{dietz2009}'s RAER analysis. One of the possible reasons that equipment based actions scored higher in the RAER analysis is due to the basis of the two studies: for one, one time replacements become automatically 'adopted' permanently almost every time after implementation. Recurring actions suffer from consistent human input, which is inconsistent. This strengthens the case in accounting for long term adoption with recommendations.

Current routines, usually heuristic choices, resolve everyday decisions quickly but grow unreliable as time pressure and information load rise \cite{bobadilla2018}. Time pressure and mental load lead to satisficing: selecting the first acceptable option rather than evaluating trade-offs. Status quo bias keeps households locked into habitual schedules, even when shifting usage windows saves money \cite{gill2022}. Decision assistance tools impose a workflow tax on users: introducing any kind of decision-making tool, digital or otherwise, decreases the likelihood of long-term adoption \cite{ariely2001}. Natural-language interfaces can reduce this cognitive tax. In building performance, natural-language platforms like EPlus-LLM extract building parameters to cut modeling time by 95\% \cite{jiang2024eplus}, and context-aware recommendation engines that explain the financial and environmental trade-offs behind a suggestion increase user acceptance by 20\% \cite{zharova2024}.

\subsection{LLM Numeric Reliability and MCDA Integration}

For arithmetic, LLMs ``often provide incorrect answers'' unless self-checked against multiple generated solution paths \cite{imani2023mathprompter}. Expecting LLMs to accurately understand how competing (and often similar) alternatives can have one clear winner is therefore unreliable without external grounding. However, testing a lone-LLM scorer is useful to see exactly how unreliable LLMs are without that grounding. The most conventional method of LLM assistance is few-shot prompting, which is when the prompt includes examples similar to the solution needed. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) can do that using a database of examples by injecting external facts into an LLM's context to reduce hallucination in open-domain question answering (QA) \cite{lewis2020}. An MCDA scoring task is closer to Case-Based Reasoning, which treats problem-solving as retrieving similar prior cases and reusing their recorded solutions \cite{aamodtplaza1994}. \citet{minorkaucher2024} found that retrieved example explanations used as in-context demonstrations improve LLM-generated outputs over zero-shot prompting.

However, both implementations have fundamental flaws. \citet{zheng2023judging} formalized the broader LLM-as-judge paradigm, in which a model's own generated scores substitute for human ratings; subsequent audits complicate its reliability for the multi-alternative comparison an MCDA scorer performs. \citet{wang2024fairescalers} show that LLMs exhibit positional bias when scoring, ranking the first response higher simply because of its placement (a problem with ranking alternatives), and LLMs also have inaccurate score calibration. \citet{li2026scoringbias} document systematic scoring-bias distortions in LLM-as-a-judge pipelines that persist even in advanced models. Based on this prior work, an unaided LLM scorer's criterion scores would be expected to cluster into a narrow middle band, missing the sharp, non-linear penalties in the reference value functions, dragging down Kendall's tau even when scores are directionally reasonable.
An alternative to having the LLM score directly, estimating the missing engineering parameters and letting a deterministic calculator handle the scoring, remains untested in this literature.

These reliability concerns motivate a closer look at how the MCDA literature itself has begun to incorporate LLMs. An MCDA problem specifies a finite set of alternatives evaluated against multiple criteria, each carrying a weight that reflects its relative importance to the decision-maker. This formalization traces to decision theory's effort to reconcile competing objectives that cannot be reduced to a single metric \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. The classical toolkit spans several method families beyond MAVT: the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) derives weights from pairwise comparisons, the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) ranks alternatives by distance from an ideal and an anti-ideal point, and outranking methods such as ELECTRE and PROMETHEE compare alternatives pairwise without collapsing them into a single index \cite{huang2011}. These methods have since been applied across domains including energy planning, environmental risk management, and healthcare resource allocation \cite{huang2011}.

MAVT aggregates single-attribute value functions into a unified score using criterion weights. However, nominal importance weights often fail to reflect effective trade-offs unless scaled by realized attribute ranges within a choice set \cite{vonnitzsch1993}. Before LLMs, MCDA researchers addressed subjective imprecision through fuzzy-set extensions of classical methods: fuzzy AHP and fuzzy TOPSIS represent decision-maker judgments as fuzzy numbers rather than crisp weights \cite{zadeh1965}. The uncertainty is formalized, but a human still has to supply the judgment. While MCDA methods traditionally require manual elicitation of parameters and preferences from domain experts, recent studies explore large language models to automate preference elicitation, alternative generation, or direct scoring \cite{svoboda2024,icaart26}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec2}

This section covers the MAVT reference framework, the three LLM-integration architectures under comparison, and the metrics used to score them. Table~\ref{tab:item1} lists the notation used throughout this paper.

\subsection{Reference Framework}

\subsubsection{MAVT Framework \& Weighting Design}
\label{sec:mavt-design}
MAVT compares alternatives on different criteria based on the range of values that occurs for each criterion \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}.

The weights are held constant across all decision types, all architectures, and the reference calculator. We did this so that any difference in score quality between decision types is because of the input accuracy and value function, not because of a difference in the way each criterion is weighted for the three decision types. Normally, the MAVT additive form (Eq.~\ref{eq:mavt_sum}) requires preferential independence among criteria \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}. Preferential independence holds when the decision maker's preference ordering over outcomes that differ only on a subset of criteria does not depend on the fixed levels of the remaining criteria; the trade-offs the decision maker is willing to make among a group of criteria remain the same regardless of how the other criteria are set \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mavt_sum}
s_j = \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i \cdot v_i(x_{ij})
\end{equation}

In our dataset the energy cost and environmental impact criteria are not statistically independent: Spearman's $\rho$ between them across the 195 test scenarios is 0.92. This is not, however, a violation of the condition the additive form requires. Preferential independence constrains the preference structure, not the empirical correlation between attribute values in a particular set of alternatives \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. A household willing to accept a given increment of emissions for a given saving in cost at 20\,$^\circ$F would accept the same trade at 90\,$^\circ$F, so the additive form remains admissible at $\rho = 0.92$. The correlation does, however, raise a distinct and legitimate concern: redundancy, or double counting, which arises if the two criteria are operationally the same quantity under different names, causing their combined 65\% weight to be applied twice to a single underlying attribute \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}. The extent to which this occurs differs sharply by decision type. Because ranking is performed within a scenario, the relevant quantity is the within-scenario association between the two criteria across the three alternatives.

For the Appliance decision type the two criteria separate. The price period is set by the utility's time-of-use window (for PECO, 2--6\,p.m.\ on weekdays \cite{peco2021}) while the emissions period is set by the PJM system-wide peak of 7\,a.m.--11\,p.m., so a cycle run at 8\,a.m.\ is off-peak for cost and on-peak for emissions. The magnitudes differ as sharply as the windows (the peak-to-off-peak rate and emissions differentials are reported in Supplementary Material S1). For HVAC and Shower they do not separate. In both, the parameters that distinguish the emissions or water quantity from the cost quantity (the occupancy-resolved emissions factor for HVAC, the flow rate and heater setpoint for Shower) are scenario-level and therefore identical across the three alternatives. Within any one scenario the environmental quantity is then a fixed scalar multiple of the cost quantity. The within-scenario Spearman correlation is 1.000 for 101 of the 105 HVAC scenarios (test and RAG corpora combined; the four exceptions have a constant raw cost across the three alternatives, so $\rho$ is undefined) and for all 80 Shower scenarios, and after normalization the two criterion scores differ by a mean of only 0.006 (HVAC) and 0.053 (Shower) on the 0--1 scale. For these two decision types the four-criterion specification therefore operates as a three-criterion one, with an effective weight of 0.65 on a single cost/emissions dimension. This is a property of the specification that is a byproduct of available data for the reference model. The two would diverge on a grid whose marginal emissions intensity varies independently of retail price, which the PJM footprint studied here does not (Section~\ref{sec:calculator-simplifications}).

In our study, the benchmark harness uses the following weight configuration: Environmental Impact (35\%), Energy Cost (30\%), Comfort (20\%), and Practicality (15\%), as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:item3}. The vector is an input held constant across architectures and decision types so that the comparison is controlled, and results are reported across multiple weight configurations, including the baseline, equal weights, and objective weight vectors derived by entropy and by MEREC (Method based on the Removal Effects of Criteria) \cite{merec2021} in the weight robustness (Appendix~\ref{app:weights}).

The Environmental Impact weight of 0.35 reflects the sizable share of national CO\textsubscript{2} emissions attributable to the residential sector \cite{dietz2009} and Value--belief--norm theory's finding that environmental orientation is the most consistent normative driver across pro-environmental household behaviours \cite{stern1999}. Additionally, tests based on entropy-based normalization of the scenario distributions independently give high weight to this criterion \cite{roszkowska2026}, though these weights are computed on post-value-function scores and are therefore descriptive rather than normative (full computation in \ref{app:weights}). Energy Cost is weighted at 0.30, just below environmental impact, because cost is the strongest predictor of consumer adoption (energy-label cost savings outperform actual cost and emissions information in shaping behavior \cite{newell2014}), and households respond to billing-rate signals across all three decision types: thermostat setpoints determine electricity draw, appliance scheduling shifts cost sharply with time-of-use rates (peak-to-off-peak savings detailed in Supplementary Material S1), and shower energy cost is heavily affected by the visibility bias documented by Attari et al. \cite{attari2010,reu2016,harrispoll2024}.

Comfort is weighted at 0.20, reflecting its role as the second driver of HVAC behavior \cite{aqilah2022} via ASHRAE\,55 setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020}. For appliances, it follows demand-response acceptance research on tolerance for scheduling delay \cite{paetz2012,waseem2020}, and for showers it peaks near the REU2016 average duration and penalizes heater setpoints below CDC Legionella thresholds \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026}. Practicality, weighted at 0.15, captures the feasibility and sustainability of an alternative rather than a household's unconstrained preference, since low adoption is a longer term effect of friction \cite{ariely2001}; it penalizes rather than eliminates infeasible options since most options would become infeasible if they were repeated for extended periods, which is not guaranteed. A 20-minute shower in a four-person household with a 50-gallon tank is the clearest case: at 2.5\,GPM the four showers together draw about 155 gallons of hot water against the roughly 40 gallons the tank can usefully supply, so the household runs out. The Shower calculator registers this by subtracting a fixed capacity penalty from practicality. By requiring a score or parameter estimate for each of the four criteria, the structured framework prevents the LLM from arbitrarily selecting an alternative based on a single dominant attribute, a known problem with LLM judgment \cite{wang2024fairescalers}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:norm_x}
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for increasing criteria}\\[4mm]
\dfrac{x_{\max} - x}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for decreasing criteria}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Energy cost and environmental impact enter as raw physical quantities (dollars, and lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2} or gallons of water); comfort and practicality are emitted by the calculators already on a 0--1 utility scale, so for those two $x$ is that score rather than a physical measurement. Each criterion's raw quantity is first converted to a direction-adjusted normalized value $\tilde{x}$, so that higher values are always better:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linear_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \tilde{x}
\end{equation}

The environmental and energy cost functions use the linear value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:linear_vf}) because equal absolute changes in these quantities represent equal improvements: a 1\,lb\,CO\textsubscript{2} reduction is equally valuable regardless of the baseline, and a \$0.10 saving carries the same decision weight at any cost level relative to the household's budget range \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}. Framing environmental impact in absolute physical units (lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2}) means the same logic is applicable \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:log_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \frac{\ln\!\left(1 + \alpha \cdot \tilde{x}\right)}{\ln(1 + \alpha)}
\end{equation}

The comfort and practicality criteria use the logarithmic value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:log_vf}) because both exhibit diminishing marginal returns: initial improvements from an uncomfortable or impractical baseline yield disproportionately large perceived gains, while incremental improvements near the optimum matter progressively less. For comfort, moving from a temperature far outside the ASHRAE~55 band toward the neutral setpoint produces large subjective gains, but further refinement within an already acceptable range has little additional effect \cite{ashrae55_2020}. For practicality, adopting a behavior that fits within budget and routine constraints is a large step; further reducing friction beyond an already feasible option adds minimal value \cite{ariely2001}. The shape parameters $\alpha = 1.5$ for comfort and $\alpha = 1.2$ for practicality were set a priori to introduce mild concavity and partially adjusted to ensure a lack of clamping (e.g. values that jump out of the 0-1 range). No elicitation was performed (e.g., midvalue splitting), and the values are not derived from data. Moderate gains (e.g., moving from the 25th to the 50th percentile of the criterion range) receive meaningful credit without over-rewarding marginal further improvements, while keeping the function close enough to linear that the difference in weights remains the dominant driver of the final MAVT score. Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity} reports the architecture ordering across the $\alpha$ sweep.

\subsection{Architecture Design and Robustness Testing}

We implement three architectures to answer the accuracy research question through a controlled comparison, in which the architecture is the experimental condition and every other factor is held fixed. The three conditions are direct scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$), example-guided scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$), and hybrid reference scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$). Across all three conditions we hold constant the criterion weights, the scenario set, the temperature, and the underlying model within each run, so that the architecture is the only factor that varies. The following subsections give the three implementations first, then the robustness suites that test whether the resulting ordering depends on design choices made inside the architectures.

\subsubsection{Architecture Implementations}
\label{sec:architectures}
All three architectures call the same model per test via OpenRouter at temperature 0.3, with model held constant within each run. The study evaluates four models spanning a range of capability, reasoning support, and cost: GPT-OSS-20B (reasoning, low effort), Qwen 3.5 9B (non-reasoning), DeepSeek V4 Flash (hybrid reasoning), and Gemini 3.5 Flash (reasoning, minimal effort), each set to its lowest available reasoning tier (OpenRouter model identifiers and routing variant in Supplementary Material S1). Alternative order in the prompt was fixed in scenario-sheet order across all shipped runs; Section~\ref{sec:alternative-order} reports the test of whether that choice matters. Ties in the MAVT aggregate were broken deterministically by the priority order environmental, energy cost, comfort, practicality (full tie-rate breakdown by model and architecture in Supplementary Material S1).

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{ArchitectureFlowcharts.png}
  \caption{Processing pipeline for each architecture.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The three architecture implementations, diagrammed in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, are described below.



The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ architecture issues one API call per alternative (three calls per scenario) using a calibrated system prompt that combines role-prompting, structured-output constraints \cite{icaart26}, and explicit bias-mitigation language drawn from the NIST framework for identifying and managing AI bias \cite{nistsp1270}. Beyond the scenario description, the prompt supplies no external context. 
The system prompt establishes the analyst role and, for each of the three decision types, gives each of the four criteria a short anchor phrase describing what a good and a poor score look like on that criterion. It closes with a strict JSON-only output instruction and a reminder to distinguish between criteria, consistent with the bias-mitigation language cited above (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1).
The user prompt provides the scenario context and asks the model to score one alternative at a time. 

The anchors are deliberately qualitative rather than numeric. For HVAC and Shower, each criterion is described by a good/moderate/poor triple stated in terms of the physical situation. The Appliance anchors reference the off-peak and peak rate periods and the overnight minimum of the grid-emissions profile, coarser domain information than the HVAC and Shower anchors carry. Supplying no anchors at all leaves the 0--1 scale undefined, which is the condition under which LLM scorers exhibit the strongest scoring bias and compress their outputs into a narrow band \cite{li2026scoringbias,nistsp1270}. The verbal triple provides some assistance on the direction and the endpoints of each scale without fixing its calibration. The technique was inspired by the triangular fuzzy numbers used to encode linguistic judgements in fuzzy MCDA \cite{zadeh1965}, which was not implemented due to its resemblance to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$. Neither prompt states any Pennsylvania-specific quantity: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is given the location and must supply the PECO time-of-use tiers and the PJM marginal emissions factors from its own knowledge.

The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ architecture retrieves the single most similar scenario ($k=1$) from a ChromaDB vector store before scoring, using the sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model. The retrieval index is built once from the 90 RAG-only scenarios described in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}; test scenarios are used only as query vectors and the index stores only RAG scenarios, not tested scenarios \cite{lewis2020}. Like $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ uses three API calls per scenario, one per alternative. The retrieved exemplar is rendered as a full worked example: its household-reported parameters, its engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle, tank size, heater setpoint, etc.), and each of its alternatives with the four reference criterion scores plus the MAVT aggregate and rank. The exemplar's engineering values are shown so the model can see how those values map to scores. The model is instructed to use the exemplar as reference but to score the target scenario independently.

The system prompt keeps $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s analyst role, JSON-only output format, and criteria-distinction instruction, but replaces the twelve per-decision-type anchor descriptions with four one-line statements of each criterion's direction: lower cost, lower emissions, higher comfort, easier implementation. The retrieved exemplar supplies scoring guidance in the anchors' place (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1). The user prompt presents the target scenario, the alternative to score, and the retrieved exemplar (with household-reported parameters, engineering values, and the four reference scores plus MAVT aggregate and rank), instructing the model to ``consider how this specific alternative performs given the scenario context.''

The retrieval count $k=1$ was selected from a development-set ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation-methods}) as the cheapest configuration not shown to be worse; DeepSeek is the one model where $k=3$ measurably wins (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}).

The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ architecture issues a single API call per scenario for structured parameter extraction. Known household-reported scenario fields (outdoor temperature, square footage, insulation tier, household size, housing type, utility budget, and location) are passed directly to the calculator without LLM involvement, and the three alternatives are taken verbatim from the scenario sheet. The LLM is asked only to estimate the genuinely withheld engineering parameters that are absent from the household-reported description. For HVAC, the wall R-value, SEER rating, and HVAC age, with the occupancy context available from the question text. For Appliance, the kWh/cycle, which comes from the appliance type, and current baseline time, which is also inferrable from the question text. For Shower, the numeric GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint.

The extracted JSON is checked to make sure it is not impossible before execution. A validation script checks extracted parameters against physical limits: HVAC wall R-value (1.0--60.0), SEER (6.0--30.0), equipment age (0.0--60.0 years); appliance energy (0.05--10.0 kWh/cycle); shower flow rate (0.5--8.0 GPM), tank capacity (10.0--120.0 gallons), and water heater setpoint (80.0--160.0\,$^\circ\text{F}$). If an LLM predicts an out-of-range value or returns non-numeric text, the script marks the extraction as a failure. Validated parameters are merged with the known fields and passed to the decision type's corresponding reference calculator.

The extraction prompt uses decomposed prompting to separate natural-language understanding from quantitative computation: unlike $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, it does not ask the LLM to score alternatives, only to extract the withheld engineering parameters into a fixed per-decision-type JSON schema, with an explicit instruction to estimate rather than omit any mandatory field (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1). The calculators $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ invokes are the same modules that generated the reference scores; $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ceiling is therefore the reference itself, with its error confined to parameter estimation. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ LLM never parses the alternatives; the calculator does, and the \texttt{default\_params} floor condition of the parameter-source ablation (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}) is the control that demonstrates how much of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s accuracy comes from the calculator alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}). Because these parameters are scenario-level, extraction errors largely cancel in ranking, so score-level error (MAE/RMSE) and appliance baseline time are the measures that reveal extraction quality (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}).

Three ablation suites test whether the reported architecture ordering depends on design choices made inside the architectures rather than on the architectures themselves. Each suite varies one design dimension at a time against the shipped configuration, holding criterion weights, temperature, and the scenario set fixed. The prompt and RAG suites share one statistical implementation, and the parameter-provenance suite's two-condition comparison is tested directly, without an omnibus (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}). Within each stratum a Friedman omnibus is computed per metric, the resulting family of omnibus tests is Holm-corrected, and only the strata whose corrected omnibus survives proceed to post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Cliff's $\delta$ accompanies each surviving pair as an effect size, and percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals are computed per configuration within each stratum independently of the Friedman outcome, since they describe single-configuration uncertainty, not a comparison. Significance is tested within each model; pooling would confound the effect under test with whichever model happens to be stronger (full test-family bookkeeping in Supplementary Material S2).

All three suites cover the same four models. Gemini's cells use three runs against the five or ten the other three models use, because its output pricing is roughly fifty times theirs; its run-to-run variance is the lowest of the four models, so three runs resolve its cell means at the precision reported (full variance figures in Supplementary Material S2).

\subsubsection{Prompt ablation}
\label{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}
The prompt ablation tests whether the reported ordering reflects the design of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ or the particular prompts used to implement them. Because that question concerns the shipped comparison, this suite runs on the same 195 Test scenarios that produced it rather than on the 90-scenario RAG dataset. Three perturbations modify one prompt property at a time against the shipped control: removing the anchors that define the endpoints of the scoring scale (the unanchored variant), inserting a chain-of-thought scaffold that instructs the model to reason before emitting its scores (the reasoning-scaffold variant), and rescaling the response range from 0--1 to 0--10 with scores divided by ten before scoring (the rescaled-response variant). $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ ships without anchors, so the unanchored variant applies to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ alone and the matrix has 28 architecture--model--variant cells, not 32. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is excluded because its LLM call extracts engineering parameters rather than emitting criterion scores; none of the three manipulations has a counterpart in its prompt. Cells run five times per model, or three for Gemini. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ control and unanchored cells were extended to ten runs for DeepSeek, GPT-OSS and Qwen after a five-run pass left the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$/Qwen unanchored comparison against control close enough to the significance threshold ($\alpha = 0.05$) to warrant more power before drawing a conclusion either way (full run counts in Supplementary Material S2); Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation} reports how it resolved.

Point estimates use the per-scenario aggregation defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}: each run produces one Kendall's $\tau$ and one Top-1 value per scenario; these are averaged across runs to give one value per variant, architecture, model and scenario, and significance is tested across the 195 scenarios within each architecture--model stratum. Using the main results' aggregation keeps the ablation's point estimates comparable to the main architecture-comparison numbers. The harness separately computes a run-to-run standard deviation per variant, treating runs rather than scenarios as the unit of analysis; that statistic describes how much a single deployment run is expected to vary by chance and is not the main quantity tested here.

\subsubsection{RAG ablation}
\label{sec:rag-ablation-methods}
The RAG ablation runs on the complete 90-scenario RAG dataset under a leave-one-out protocol: each scenario serves as the query with itself excluded from retrieval. The dataset supplies both the query set and the retrieval index here because the manipulations under test change the index itself, and the dataset scenarios are the only ones carrying reference scores in both roles. Seven configurations are compared against the $k=3$ control of the time: retrieval $k$ at 1 and at 5, an embedding swap replacing all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with the smaller paraphrase-MiniLM-L3-v2, exemplars with the hidden engineering parameters removed, exemplar descriptions with the reference scores and ranks removed, random dataset draws in place of similarity-ranked exemplars, and an offline \texttt{nearest-neighbor} configuration that assigns the retrieved scenario's scores directly with no LLM call.

$k=1$ and $k=5$ test whether a single high-similarity exemplar suffices and whether broader context improves or dilutes the signal; the embedding swap tests how much encoder quality matters. The exemplar-content configurations separate the engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle) from the household-reported labels, and the score-anchoring configurations test whether the model uses the reference scores as calibration anchors or as noise. Two configurations are controls: random exemplars for retrieval quality, and \texttt{nearest-neighbor} for whether unmodified exemplar copying carries ranking signal on its own.

\subsubsection{Parameter-provenance ablation}
\label{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}
The third suite isolates how much of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy comes from the LLM's parameter extraction step, as opposed to a deterministic calculator scoring the alternatives on its own (motivation and full results in Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}). It also runs on the 195 Test scenarios. Both conditions feed the same reference calculators used to build the reference model (Section~\ref{sec:gt-calculators}), are scored against the ranking those calculators produce from each scenario's true engineering values, and differ only in where the withheld engineering parameters come from. \texttt{extracted} supplies the values $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s LLM returned for each scenario, read from the existing per-model result files rather than by re-querying any model. \texttt{default\_params} supplies one fixed value per hidden parameter, the dataset median for numeric parameters and the dataset mode for categorical ones, reused for every scenario with no per-scenario inference at all, making it the floor a calculator-only system would reach if extraction contributed nothing beyond a constant guess.

The withheld parameters match those described for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ in Section~\ref{sec:architectures}: R-value, SEER, HVAC age, and occupancy context for HVAC; appliance type, kWh/cycle, and baseline time for Appliance; GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint for Shower. The \texttt{extracted} against \texttt{default\_params} contrast isolates per-scenario inference: the gap between them is what extraction contributes over a no-inference baseline. Every condition is computed from data already collected for the main benchmark. A scenario whose extraction failed, through a non-JSON response, a missing parameter, or a value outside the calculator's physical validation bounds (Section~\ref{sec:architectures}), is excluded from the extracted condition's metrics for that scenario rather than backfilled with a default, following the sentinel-exclusion convention used throughout this paper (Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}). The full results table (Supplementary Material S2) reports the resulting scenario counts per condition in its notes.

\subsubsection{Alternative ordering}
\label{sec:alternative-order}

Language models weight response options by position as well as by content
\cite{wang2024fairescalers,zheng2023judging}, so a fixed alternative order could introduce bias. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ score one alternative per
API call. Each call names the alternative under evaluation, states the scenario
context, and lists the remaining two alternatives on a single line for context.
The calls are independent and carry no conversation state, so the sequence in
which they are issued cannot affect any individual response. Reversing the
alternative order therefore changes one thing the model can observe:
the order of a two-element list mentioned in passing. When scoring a setpoint of
76\,\textdegree F, for example, the prompt moves from ``Other alternatives
available for this decision: [72, 80]'' to ``[80, 72]'' while the alternative
being scored, the scenario context, and the scoring instructions are
byte-identical. Neither architecture ever presents the three alternatives as a ranked list to choose among, which is the presentation format the position-bias literature concerns itself with. 

In every condition, reversing the order means reversing the three alternative values as they appear in the scenario record, so that the value listed first becomes the value listed last. For $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ this surfaces only in the context line described above. We rescored all 195 test scenarios in reversed order for each of the four models and compared each reversed run against that model's five shipped runs on top-1 correctness (per-model shift and significance in Section~\ref{sec:res-order}). Because the ranking tie-break resolves exact ties by input order, reversal can flip a fully tied choice set on its own, which biases the comparison toward detecting an effect and makes the observed null conservative. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s shipped runs disagree with each other on top-1 for 12\% to 48\% of scenarios depending on the model, so the effect is bounded, not excluded. With no material difference between the reversed and shipped conditions, the shipped-order runs were retained as the reported ones (per-model McNemar statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is not covered by that argument. Its extraction call passes the whole scenario record, including all three alternatives; reversal therefore changes the order in which the model reads them, not a list mentioned in passing. Any effect would be indirect, acting through the estimated physical parameters rather than through a score, because the calculator and not the model produces the ranking. We therefore re-ran $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction over all 195 test scenarios in reversed order, five runs per model, alongside three runs in the shipped order collected in the same session; together with the five original shipped runs, each model contributes eight shipped-order runs against five reversed, 13 in all. In every condition the extracted parameters were passed to the calculator with the alternatives restored to canonical order, so the ranking tie-break cannot manufacture a difference between conditions. We test whether the reversed runs are distinguishable from the shipped-order runs by an exact permutation test that reassigns the reversed label across all $\binom{13}{5} = 1287$ ways of choosing five of the 13 runs. Unlike a paired test on disagreements, this uses every scenario in every run, so its floor is $p = 1/1287$.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}
Accuracy is evaluated on scoring and ranking. Score error uses Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) between architecture scores and reference scores on the 0--1 MAVT scale, computed separately for each criterion then aggregated across criteria. Ranking accuracy uses Kendall's $\tau$ between the architecture's induced ranking of the three alternatives and Top-1 accuracy (whether the architecture's best-ranked alternative matches the reference ranking). Each per-scenario $\tau$ is averaged across the 195 scenarios by arithmetic mean. With $n=3$ alternatives, per-scenario $\tau$ can take only discrete values ($-1, -\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, 1$) before averaging. A uniformly random ranking gives Top-1 accuracy of $1/3$ and expected Kendall's $\tau$ of $0$. Efficiency is evaluated using total tokens (input plus output) and cost, both summed across the 195-scenario test set.

One alternative failure-handling rule appears in the appendices for comparison. Imputation (Supplementary Material S2) works per run: scenarios that fail receive the 0.5 scale midpoint for the affected criterion scores before that run's metrics are computed, as opposed to dropping the scenario in overall metric calculation.

The paired Wilcoxon comparisons treat the scenario as the unit, computing each of the 195 scenarios' metrics from the mean over the runs in which that scenario was scored successfully, which is five for most scenarios and fewer where a run failed. The paired bootstrap confidence intervals resample scenarios while carrying all successful runs of a drawn scenario together. The paired Wilcoxon tests on the main architecture comparison are Holm-corrected across a single 56-test family; the 24 comparisons on the three reported primary metrics ($\tau$, MAE, and Top-1) remain significant at the 0.05 level (full test-family enumeration in Supplementary Material S2). Models are not pooled, since each is a separate test.

\section{Decision Types and Scenario Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
Three decision types were selected for their high behavioral plasticity and RAER: HVAC (adjusting thermostat setpoints), Appliance (time-of-use [TOU] scheduling), and Shower (duration). HVAC was prioritized because space heating and cooling account for approximately a third of global residential energy consumption and grow as household incomes rise \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}. The scenario dataset has two pools. The test set contains 195 scenarios (70 HVAC, 65 Appliance, and 60 Shower); each scenario presents three alternatives scored independently by each architecture and by the reference calculator. The RAG scenarios (detailed in Supplementary Material S1) contain an additional 90 scenarios (35 HVAC, 35 Appliance, and 20 Shower) that populate the retrieval index used by the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ architecture; RAG scenarios are used only in the retrieval index \cite{lewis2020}. The scenario counts were chosen to ensure full coverage of the parameter space rather than to satisfy a statistical power calculation. Each decision type's scenario set crosses the categorical parameters (insulation tier, housing type, appliance type, flow-rate band) with representative numeric draws (outdoor temperature, square footage, household size, utility budget), with coverage of every categorical cross-cell ensured by a combinatorial audit (detailed in Supplementary Material S1). The 90 RAG scenarios follow the same parameter distribution without excess scenarios in any combination and are kept fully disjoint from the test set, so no test scenario shares an identical parameter signature with a retrieval-index entry.

The test dataset withholds each scenario's exact engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle, occupancy flags) behind homeowner-accessible labels for every architecture, while $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s retrieved RAG exemplars still expose their own engineering values as worked examples (Table~\ref{tab:parameter_comparison}; Section~\ref{sec:architectures}). The reference calculators in this study were built on Pennsylvania-specific data (electricity and natural gas rates, utility structures, and PJM marginal emission factors). The models tested were made aware of this through the locations given.

\subsection{Reference Calculators}
\label{sec:gt-calculators}

The reference ranges in Table~\ref{table:reference_ranges} are computed as the 5th--95th percentiles of the reference cost distributions over the scenario dataset rather than extreme theoretical limits, so that normalization preserves meaningful spread across typical residential alternatives. The HVAC environmental bounds are derived from the corresponding energy percentile envelope through the PJM marginal emission factors, as detailed below, while the Appliance environmental bounds are percentiles of the realized per-cycle emissions distribution; the comfort and practicality bounds are constructed score bounds (0.0--1.0 and 0.05--1.0), not percentiles. The practicality lower bound of 0.05 instead of 0.0 reflects the design assumption that no household alternative is truly impossible to execute: even the most impractical option (e.g., a 20-minute shower for four occupants sharing a small tank) remains physically performable, merely highly inadvisable; a residual floor prevents the value function from treating any alternative as categorically infeasible \cite{ariely2001}. These bounds draw on actual Pennsylvania setpoints and therefore are still based on real-world usage. Using dataset percentiles rather than the full theoretical physics envelope is a choice which comes at the cost of truncating scores for extreme outlier scenarios. The endpoints below are reported to the precision shown in the table.

For HVAC, cost percentiles reflect active-conditioning alternatives. A setpoint near outdoor temperature produces zero decision-period load and collapses to \$0, which makes the lower bound go to zero. Cost normalization uses a bundled Pennsylvania retail rate of \$0.19/kWh. EIA reports a lower state average of about \$0.177/kWh \cite{eia_faq_electricity2024}, but that figure is an average revenue per kWh over all residential customers in the state, including those on legacy supply contracts. The rate used here is instead the all-in amount a household on default service pays at the margin: generation supply plus transmission, distribution, and the customer-assistance and energy-efficiency riders that appear as separate line items on the bill, priced against the default-service rates of the six Pennsylvania utilities modelled here \cite{paupc2025}. Excluding zero-load points, the realized 8-hour cost distribution at this rate spans \$0.38--\$3.29. An Off option receives top cost score via decreasing-value normalization. Using this kWh distribution with PJM marginal emissions factors (0.976 off-peak, 1.041 peak lbs CO\textsubscript{2}/kWh) yields HVAC environmental bounds of 1.96--18.04 lbs CO\textsubscript{2} \cite{pjm2022emissions}.


For appliances, the realized per-cycle cost distribution (kWh/cycle multiplied by the resolved time-of-use rate across the six PA utilities) spans \$0.025--\$0.71, and the realized per-cycle emissions distribution (kWh/cycle through the PJM marginal factor at run time) spans 0.288--3.643 lbs CO\textsubscript{2}. The underlying per-cycle energy endpoints remain consistent with the ENERGY STAR certified-products distribution (efficient HE washers near 0.25\,kWh) and include the older non-certified electric resistance dryers \cite{energystar_products,neep2015}.

For showers, the realized per-shower cost distribution spans \$0.14--\$1.14. Shower energy follows the mixing-fraction physics targeting a fixed 105\,\textdegree{}F delivery temperature, so it depends only on the rise from the mains inlet (seasonal inlet temperatures from NREL hot-water modeling \cite{burch2007}) to that target and is independent of heater setpoint for setpoints at or above the 105\,\textdegree{}F delivery target, which all dataset setpoints (105--140\,\textdegree{}F) satisfy. Shower environmental impact is defined as water volume (gallons); the realized distribution of flow rate multiplied by duration spans 6.0--45.0 gallons at the 5th--95th percentile, with flow-rate and duration benchmarks from EPA WaterSense Appendix~B \cite{epawatersense}, REU2016 \cite{reu2016}, and The Harris Poll \cite{harrispoll2024}. Water volume was chosen because it is the major environmental footprint of showering, and showers account for a major share of indoor residential water use. However, this means that models are tested on slightly different grounds between decision types.

The endpoints above were obtained by running every dataset scenario through its decision type's calculator, test and retrieval scenarios alike, pooling the raw quantity across all three alternatives of every scenario of that type, and taking the 5th and 95th percentiles of the pooled distribution. For HVAC, alternatives carrying zero conditioning load are dropped from the pool. The normalization floor is therefore set by the cheapest actively conditioning option rather than by a degenerate zero. The one endpoint not read off a realized distribution is the HVAC environmental bound, which is converted from the HVAC energy percentile envelope through the PJM factors as described above, so that the cost and emissions scales for that decision type stay physically consistent with each other. The endpoints are then held as constants inside each calculator's value function rather than recomputed at run time, so that a later change to the scenario dataset cannot move the normalization without the change being made explicitly.

Each reference calculator takes a scenario with three alternatives and returns four scores per alternative plus the raw physical quantities for environmental impact and energy cost before value-function transformation (not used in any architecture scoring). Energy cost is in dollars per 8-hour decision period (HVAC), per cycle (Appliance), or per shower (Shower); monthly estimates are computed only for the budget penalty; Environmental Impact is in lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2} for HVAC and Appliance decisions and in gallons of water for Shower decisions.

For HVAC and Appliance decisions, environmental impact uses PJM marginal emissions factors:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \mathrm{CO_2} = E_{\mathrm{kWh}} \cdot \varepsilon(t), \\[4pt]
  \varepsilon(t) = \begin{cases}
    1.041 \text{ lbs/kWh} & \text{peak (7am--11pm)} \\
    0.976 \text{ lbs/kWh} & \text{off-peak}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:emissions}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $E_{\mathrm{kWh}}$ is the decision-period energy use and $\varepsilon(t)$ is the PJM marginal emissions factor at run time $t$ \cite{pjm2022emissions}. Marginal factors are used rather than averages because shifting a residential load displaces or adds the generator at the margin.
All three calculators apply an identical four-tier budget penalty $P(u)$ multiplicatively to the energy-cost value-function output, where $u = C_{\mathrm{monthly}} / B$ is monthly cost utilization relative to the household's stated budget $B$:
\begin{equation}
  P(u) = \begin{cases}
    1.0                          & u < 0.80                   \\[4pt]
    1 - 2.5\,(u - 0.80)         & 0.80 \le u < 1.0           \\[4pt]
    0.5\,e^{-3(u - 1.0)}        & 1.0 \phantom{0}\le u < 1.5 \\[4pt]
    0                            & u \ge 1.5
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:budget_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $P(u)$ is a unitless multiplicative penalty on the (post value-function) energy-cost score, $u$ is monthly budget utilization, $C_{\mathrm{monthly}}$ is estimated monthly energy/water-related cost for the decision type, and $B$ is the household-stated monthly budget for utilities.
\noindent The curve's shape (a no-penalty zone, a decline approaching the limit, loss-averse acceleration past it, and infeasibility beyond 150\%) follows the cited behavioral-economics literature in direction only; the values 0.80, 2.5 and 1.5 and the exponential form are design choices \cite{thaler1999,heath1996,prelec1998,gathergood2012}. Applying the penalty multiplicatively ensures extremely cheap or expensive alternatives cannot completely sway ranking on energy cost alone. For HVAC decisions, $C_{\mathrm{monthly}} = 90\,C_{\mathrm{8h}}$, where $C_{\mathrm{8h}}$ is the energy cost for one 8-hour conditioning period. The Appliance and Shower monthly-cost conversions follow the same pattern (full equations in Supplementary Material S1).

Thermal load is a four-component ASHRAE-style balance \cite{accamanualj} (conductive, internal-gain, solar, and infiltration, with housing-type envelope multipliers and an always-on standby baseline), divided by an effective EER derived from SEER (both rating quantities as defined in \cite{ahri2008}), then scaled by an occupancy multiplier (1.0 fully occupied, 0.75 overnight, and $1 - h_{\mathrm{away}}/48$ for $h_{\mathrm{away}}$ hours of daytime absence) to give decision-period energy use over the 8-hour window:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  Q_{\mathrm{cond}} &= \tfrac{1}{R}\,m_h A_f\,\Delta T, &
  Q_{\mathrm{inf}}  &= 1.08\,\tfrac{A_f h_c\,\mathrm{ACH}}{60}\,\Delta T, \\[2pt]
  Q_{\mathrm{int}}  &= 400\,n + A_f + 800, &
  Q_{\mathrm{sol}}  &= 20\,(0.15\,A_f),
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hvac_load}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  Q = \max\!\left(0,\;
  \begin{cases}
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} + Q_{\mathrm{int}} + Q_{\mathrm{sol}} & \text{cooling} \\[3pt]
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} - Q_{\mathrm{int}}                    & \text{heating}
  \end{cases}\right)
  \label{eq:hvac_load_total}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{Q}{1000\,\mathrm{EER}}\,H\,r(o),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{EER} = 1.12\,S - 0.02\,S^{2}
  \label{eq:hvac_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $R$ is the envelope R-value, $A_f$ the conditioned floor area (ft\textsuperscript{2}), $m_h$ the housing-type envelope multiplier (1.2 apartment and condo, 1.5 townhouse and rowhouse, 1.7 single-family), $\Delta T$ the indoor--outdoor temperature difference, $h_c$ the ceiling height, $\mathrm{ACH}=0.35$ the infiltration air-change rate, $n$ the household size, $H=8$ the decision-period length in hours, $S$ the rated SEER, and $r(o)$ the occupancy multiplier. Solar gain enters only in cooling season, and internal gain reverses sign in heating season because occupant and equipment heat offsets the load the system must supply. The 800\,BTU/hr standby term keeps a zero-$\Delta T$ setpoint from producing exactly zero load. Environmental impact applies an occupancy-resolved PJM marginal emissions factor to that energy use, using the peak/off-peak split of Eq.~\ref{eq:emissions}. Age- and maintenance-driven efficiency degradation is not applied to this energy path; it appears instead as a reliability factor on the practicality score, so the energy score reflects the setpoint choice rather than the system's condition and degradation.

Comfort uses a tent function anchored to ASHRAE\,55 PMV-optimal setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020} ($76\,^\circ$F in cooling mode, $70\,^\circ$F in heating mode), with an additional penalty term for household sizes $n > 3$:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{comfort}} = \tfrac{1}{10}\max\!\Bigl(0,\; 10 - \lvert T_{\mathrm{in}} - T^{*}\rvert - \tfrac{(n-3)\,0.3\,\lvert T_{\mathrm{in}} - T^{*}\rvert}{3}\Bigr)
  \label{eq:hvac_comfort}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $C_{\mathrm{comfort}}$ is a 0--1 comfort score, $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the indoor setpoint, and $T^{*}$ is the context-dependent optimal setpoint used as the peak of the tent function.

Practicality utility follows a piecewise value function of setpoint extremity, indoor--outdoor $\Delta T$, and equipment age. This is the first of five comfort and practicality utilities defined this way across the three calculators. The extremity slopes steepen on the least-tolerable directions, the outdoor--indoor $\Delta T$ feasibility factor is used to show operation near system limits, and the degradation change takes into account that an older or poorly maintained unit is a less practical choice to rely on:
\begin{equation}
  \delta(T_{\mathrm{in}}) =
  \begin{cases}
    1.0\,(71 - T_{\mathrm{in}}) & \text{cooling, } T_{\mathrm{in}} < 71 \\[3pt]
    1.5\,(T_{\mathrm{in}} - 82) & \text{cooling, } T_{\mathrm{in}} > 82 \\[3pt]
    1.8\,(63 - T_{\mathrm{in}}) & \text{heating, } T_{\mathrm{in}} < 63 \\[3pt]
    0.8\,(T_{\mathrm{in}} - 76) & \text{heating, } T_{\mathrm{in}} > 76 \\[3pt]
    0                             & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:hvac_practicality}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\delta(T_{\mathrm{in}})$ is the extremity penalty (score points per \textdegree{}F beyond adoption-comfort bounds); the pre-feasibility score is $\mathrm{clip}_{[0.5,\,10]}(10-\delta(T_{\mathrm{in}}))$, multiplied by an outdoor--indoor $\Delta T$ feasibility factor and by $(1-D)$, where
\begin{equation}
  D = \min\!\Bigl(0.30,\; \delta_{\mathrm{maint}}\bigl[\,1.5\min(a,10) + 0.5\max(a-10,0)\,\bigr]\Bigr)
  \label{eq:hvac_degradation}
\end{equation}
\noindent is the fraction of efficiency lost to age and maintenance (capped at 30\%), $a$ is HVAC age in years, and $\delta_{\mathrm{maint}}$ is the base annual loss rate.

An ``Off'' alternative (no active conditioning) is scored with zero decision-period energy, cost, and emissions; its comfort and practicality are instead evaluated against the free-float indoor temperature the building drifts to with the system off. These are outdoor $+5$\,\textdegree{}F in cooling season and $+10$\,\textdegree{}F in heating season, a modeling assumption reflecting solar and internal gains that hold a closed, occupied house above outdoor air temperature, not a value tabulated by \cite{ashrae55_2020,accamanualj}.

Energy cost per cycle is kWh/cycle multiplied by the location- and time-specific TOU rate, resolved to one of six Pennsylvania utilities (PECO, PPL, West Penn, Penelec, MetEd, Duquesne) \cite{peco2021,ppl2025,firstenergy2025,firstenergy2026,duquesne2026} by mapping household location to a serving utility and then that utility's peak/off-peak window. The PJM emissions window (7am--11pm) applies to environmental impact regardless of the utility's billing window, because emissions follow the regional grid. The calculator does not model weekend, holiday, or seasonal peak-window shifts, using weekday summer TOU rates year-round.

Comfort utility is parameterized by delay, appliance type, housing type, household size, and run-time hour (late-night runs, 22:00--07:00, incur a noise penalty); practicality utility by delay, run-time hour, and household coordination difficulty. Delay is the minimum circular distance from the baseline time $t_0$ on a 24-hour clock:
\begin{equation}
  d(t,t_0)=\min\!\bigl((h(t)-h(t_0))\bmod 24,\; (h(t_0)-h(t))\bmod 24\bigr),
  \label{eq:appliance_delay_circular}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $d(t,t_0)$ is delay in hours between run time $t$ and baseline time $t_0$ and $h(\cdot)$ maps a time to hour-of-day. The base comfort component is a piecewise decay from 10 at zero delay, with appliance-specific tolerance ceilings of 12\,hr (dishwashers), 8\,hr (washers), and 6\,hr (dryers). Demand-response acceptance research \cite{paetz2012,waseem2020} establishes that such ceilings exist, not where they fall:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{base}}(d)=
  \begin{cases}
    10                                  & d=0 \\[3pt]
    10-\frac{2}{3}\,d                   & 0<d\le 3 \\[3pt]
    8-\frac{1}{2}\,(d-3)                & 3<d\le 7 \\[3pt]
    6-\frac{2}{5}\,(d-7)                & 7<d\le 12 \\[3pt]
    2                                   & d>12
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_base_comfort}
\end{equation}

The late-night noise penalty scales with housing type: apartment and condo units receive the largest multiplier ($\kappa=1.5$) because shared walls transmit vibrations directly, townhouse and rowhouse an intermediate ($\kappa=1.2$), and isolated single-family homes the smallest ($\kappa=0.8$), and it is joined by a household-size penalty; \citet{waseem2020} supports the direction of that effect, not its magnitude. The penalty fires when the appliance's rated sound level exceeds 45\,dBA, which penalizes washers and dryers (74 and 65\,dBA) and leaves a dishwasher, rated at 45\,dBA, exactly at the threshold and therefore unpenalized. The threshold is a categorical design choice, not an indoor limit: the EPA/WHO 35\,dBA figure applies to sound received in a sleeping space \cite{epa1974noise}, whereas the calculator compares rated source levels and applies no attenuation model, so the two quantities are not directly comparable. Timing complexity adds a practicality penalty at run-time hours whose low-price zones sit at the brink of the day, perceived as too early or too late to act on \cite{paetz2012}.
\begin{equation}
  N(t,\mathrm{type},\mathrm{housing})=
  \begin{cases}
    0 & \text{if } h(t)\in[7,22) \text{ or } \mathrm{dBA}(\mathrm{type})\le \theta \\[3pt]
    2\cdot \kappa(\mathrm{housing}) & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_noise_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $N(t,\mathrm{type},\mathrm{housing})$ is the noise-disruption penalty in comfort-score points, $\mathrm{dBA}(\mathrm{type})$ is the appliance noise level, and $\theta = 45$\,dBA is the threshold. The household-size penalty scales with delay relative to the appliance-specific maximum acceptable delay $d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})$:
\begin{equation}
  S(d,n,\mathrm{type})=\sigma(n)\cdot \min\!\left(\frac{d}{d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})},\,1\right),
  \label{eq:appliance_size_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\sigma(n)$ steps from 0.0 for fewer than 3 occupants, to 0.8 for 3--4, to 1.5 for 5 or more. Final comfort is $C_{\mathrm{comfort}}=\mathrm{clip}_{[0,10]}(C_{\mathrm{base}}-N-S)$.

For budget utilization in \eqref{eq:budget_penalty}, the appliance calculator converts per-cycle cost to a monthly estimate using appliance-specific cycles-per-month estimates, from 215, 300, and 283 cycles/year for dishwashers, washers, and dryers respectively \cite{energystar_dishwasher_criteria2023,energystar_clotheswasher,doe_dryer_tp_2011}.

Practicality mirrors the delay schedule with additional penalties for late-night timing complexity and household coordination difficulty. The base practicality component is:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{base}}(d)=
  \begin{cases}
    10                                  & d=0 \\[3pt]
    10-d                                & 0<d\le 2 \\[3pt]
    8-\frac{3}{4}\,(d-2)                & 2<d\le 4 \\[3pt]
    6.5-\frac{1}{2}\,(d-4)              & 4<d\le 8 \\[3pt]
    4.5-\frac{3}{8}\,(d-8)              & 8<d\le 12 \\[3pt]
    1.5                                 & d>12
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_base_practicality}
\end{equation}
\noindent Timing complexity applies an additive penalty by run-time hour:
\begin{equation}
  T(t)=
  \begin{cases}
    2 & 0\le h(t)<6 \\[3pt]
    1 & 22\le h(t)<24 \\[3pt]
    0 & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:appliance_timing_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent and household coordination difficulty uses the same delay scaling form as in comfort:
\begin{equation}
  K(d,n,\mathrm{type})=\kappa_n(n)\cdot \min\!\left(\frac{d}{d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})},\,1\right),
  \label{eq:appliance_coordination_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\kappa_n(n)$ is an occupants-based factor increasing with occupants. Final practicality is $C_{\mathrm{prac}}=\mathrm{clip}_{[1.5,10]}(P_{\mathrm{base}}-T-K)$, with a daytime floor applied for $h(t)\in[7,22)$ and $d\le d_{\max}(\mathrm{type})$.

Mains inlet temperature is derived from outdoor temperature between 45\,\textdegree{}F (winter) and 65\,\textdegree{}F (summer) \cite{burch2007}, setting the fraction of flow requiring heating to reach a $105\,^\circ$F delivery target \cite{zhang2023}; shower energy and environmental impact (water volume) follow from that mixing fraction, flow rate, and duration, and energy cost applies a flat electricity rate to that energy:
\begin{equation}
  f = \min\!\left(1,\;\max\!\left(0,\;\frac{T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}{T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)\right),
  \qquad
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{f\,G\,\rho\,(T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}})\,t}{3412\,\eta}
  \label{eq:shower_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $f$ is the hot-water mixing fraction, $T_{\mathrm{d}} = 105\,^\circ$F the delivery target, $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ the mains inlet temperature, $T_{\mathrm{tank}}$ the water-heater setpoint, $G$ the flow rate in GPM, $\rho = 8.33$\,lbs/gallon, $t$ the duration in minutes, 3412 the BTU-per-kWh conversion, and $\eta = 0.92$ the electric water-heater efficiency. Because $f$ and the temperature rise $T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$ move inversely, their product reduces to $T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$, which is why shower energy depends on the inlet temperature but not on the heater setpoint for any setpoint at or above the delivery target.

Comfort takes into account duration, heater setpoint, household contention, and outdoor temperature, with a fixed penalty outside the 120--140\,\textdegree{}F heater band for mixing margin and Legionella amplification risk below it and standby loss or scald risk above it \cite{cdc2026}. The comfort-optimal duration follows the REU2016 7.8-minute average above 77\,\textdegree{}F and reaches about 10.3 minutes at the winter cap (10\% per 10.8\,\textdegree{}F drop). This cold-weather elasticity is a modeling assumption supported only in direction by the literature \cite{reu2016}, rather than a directly reported coefficient, and the cap is a cross-population extrapolation from a southern-Spain sample; warm-weather scoring is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{comfort}}(\tau,T_{\mathrm{heater}},n)
  = \mathrm{clip}_{[0,10]}\!\Bigl(C_{\mathrm{base}}(\tau)
  -\Delta_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_{\mathrm{heater}})
  -\Delta_{\mathrm{cont}}(\tau,n)\Bigr).
  \label{eq:shower_comfort_full}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $\tau$ is shower duration (min), $n$ is occupants, $C_{\mathrm{base}}(\tau)$ is the base comfort curve, $\Delta_{\mathrm{temp}}(T_{\mathrm{heater}})$ is a penalty, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{cont}}(\tau,n)$ is a household-contention penalty.

Practicality, on the other hand, takes into account duration and tank-capacity feasibility, where the hot water required is the per-shower draw multiplied by the number of occupants, so household size enters practicality through the capacity test rather than as a separate term:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{need}} = n\,\tau\,\dot V\,f_{\mathrm{hot}}, \qquad
  H_{\mathrm{avail}} = 0.8\,S.
  \label{eq:shower_capacity_need_avail}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $H_{\mathrm{need}}$ is total hot-water volume required for the household's showers over the relevant period, $H_{\mathrm{avail}}$ is available hot-water draw volume, and $S$ is nominal tank capacity (all in gallons); a fixed penalty is applied when $H_{\mathrm{need}}>H_{\mathrm{avail}}$.

For budget utilization in the budget penalty equation, shower monthly cost is estimated from per-shower cost using an average showers-per-person-per-day rate of 0.9 \cite{reu2016}. The anchor points that set the band boundaries are sourced from the 7.8-minute REU2016 average, the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold, the CDC Legionella range, and the ASHRAE\,55 comfort band \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026,ashrae55_2020}.

The penalty magnitudes and slopes between those boundaries are design choices for which the literature fixes a direction but not a size. Storage-tank studies report usable capacity ratios of 70--85\% of nominal volume across inlet configurations \cite{abdidin2024}, and the DOE first-hour rating procedure ends a draw once outlet temperature falls 15\,\textdegree{}F below its maximum \cite{shen2021}, which places the 0.80 usable-tank fraction inside a measured range without pinning it there. Storage simulations show outlet temperature dropping below a usable shower level once a long draw exhausts the hot layer \cite{marini2017}, which supports treating capacity exhaustion as a discrete failure rather than a graded decline but says nothing about the 3.0 magnitude. Metered shower durations are right-skewed with a thin upper tail \cite{wilkes2005}, which supports a practicality curve that steepens past 15 minutes without identifying its slope. Occupancy-scaled hot-water models raise both event probability and simultaneity with each added occupant \cite{rouleau2019}, but no study converts shower queuing into a per-minute satisfaction penalty, so the 0.5 per-minute contention penalty at four or more occupants carries a supported sign and an unsupported magnitude. The shower practicality curve measures likelihood of adoption, not momentary preference, and is therefore not centered on the 7.8-minute comfort optimum. Beyond the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold the curve declines conservatively: 33\% of adults self-report showers longer than 15\,min \cite{harrispoll2024}, while the metered REU2016 data implies a much lower true rate \cite{reu2016}. The duration curve is not the only route by which a long shower loses practicality: because the capacity test scales required hot water by occupancy, a duration that is unremarkable for one person can exhaust the tank for four, and the curve and the capacity penalty then compound.

Score-level error measures how well $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ reproduces the reference calculator's output, but it does not separate two distinct questions: how accurately the LLM estimates each withheld engineering parameter, and whether an estimation error changes the recommendation. Table~\ref{tab:extraction_accuracy} reports both for the seven numeric parameters. Each extracted parameter was compared against the scenario's source value across all 195 test scenarios to give the MAE column. The counterfactual column was then produced by re-running the calculator on each scenario whose extraction of that parameter was wrong, substituting that one erroneous value while holding every other parameter at its true value, and recording how often the top-ranked alternative changed. Reported as a conditional probability, it isolates the consequence of an error in one parameter from the frequency of that error.

Absolute extraction error is substantial. HVAC equipment age is mis-estimated around 6.1--8.5 years on average and shower tank size to within 6.7--8.9 gallons, both large fractions of the plausible range. The three categorical parameters are scored differently, by exact match against the scenario's source value after normalizing both sides to a canonical form, and are not in Table~\ref{tab:extraction_accuracy} because MAE requires a numeric difference, and a categorical match or mismatch has none. Appliance baseline run time is recovered exactly by all four models, and appliance type by three of the four, with Gemini missing 4 of 65 cases (93.8\%). HVAC occupancy context is the weak one at 75.4\% for GPT-OSS, 78.6\% for Qwen, 80.0\% for Gemini and 81.4\% for DeepSeek, since it must be inferred from the phrasing of the question rather than read off a field.

That error rarely propagates to the decision. For most parameters, the conditional probability that an extraction error flips the top-ranked alternative sits below 0.09, and for HVAC equipment age it is zero across all four models. This is because the LLM estimates scenario-level parameters that enter all three alternatives identically, so an error shifts the whole choice set and largely cancels in the ranking. Shower GPM is the consistent exception, carrying the highest flip probability for every model (0.133--0.233), roughly two to three times the next-largest parameter for three of the four models and about 1.5 times for Qwen. GPM is the one extracted parameter that must map a discrete homeowner-facing tier (low\_flow, standard, high\_flow) onto a continuous value that scales both energy and water volume directly, and it is the parameter behind Shower being $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s weakest decision type (Section~\ref{sec:res-bytype}).

\subsection{Isolating the Contribution of Extraction}
\label{sec:res-paramprov}
The error-cancellation mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction} raises a confound: errors that cancel in the ranking could let $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ rank well from calculator access alone, with the LLM's estimates contributing little. A parameter-source ablation separates the two (design and significance-testing methodology in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}). We conducted two conditions on the 195 test scenarios, differing only in where the withheld engineering parameters come from: the values the LLM actually returned, and a single dataset-median constant per parameter reused for every scenario. Both are scored against the ranking the reference calculators produce from the scenario's true values; the second performs no per-scenario inference at all and so isolates what calculator access alone buys. The full per-model, per-condition results table appears in Supplementary Material S2.

With no per-scenario inference, the median-parameter condition reaches $\tau = 0.641$ and Top-1 of 76.9\%, well above chance but far below the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ results. LLM extraction lifts this to $\tau = 0.880$--$0.923$ and Top-1 of 89.7--93.1\%, a gain of 0.24--0.28 in $\tau$ attributable to extraction rather than to the calculator. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Holm-corrected across the twelve model--metric tests, confirm that extraction significantly beats the dataset-median condition on all three metrics for all four models ($p_{\text{Holm}} < 10^{-3}$ throughout, Cliff's $\delta$ from $-0.14$ to $-0.24$ on Kendall's $\tau$ and Top-1 and from 0.35 to 0.49 on MAE; full statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:res-sensitivity}

To test the stability of the ranking results, both the reference ranking and the architecture rankings were recomputed under an equal-weight vector ($w_j = 0.25$ for all $j$) and entropy- and MEREC-derived vectors, applied pooled and per decision type. These objective vectors derive weights from the criterion-score variation across alternatives rather than assigning them a priori (full derivation in ~\ref{app:weights}). The full Kendall's $\tau$ matrix by model and weight vector appears in Supplementary Material S2. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ is unconditional: it holds in all 28 model $\times$ weight-vector cells, by a margin of at least $0.304$ Kendall's $\tau$.

The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ advantage over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is not unconditional. It survives the design, equal and entropy vectors in all four models, but MEREC weights narrow it substantially and in two cells reverse it: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ overtakes $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ on Kendall's $\tau$ for Gemini under both the MEREC HVAC vector ($0.676$ vs.\ $0.600$) and MEREC pooled ($0.655$ vs.\ $0.632$). Every such case is a MEREC vector, which loads onto Comfort (0.663 for HVAC against the design's 0.200), the one criterion $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ estimates about as well as $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ does (interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}; per-model results appear in Supplementary Material S2). MEREC derives a criterion's weight from how much removing it changes the alternatives' ranking, which in practice tracks that criterion's within-scenario score range: HVAC's Comfort scores swing more across a scenario's three alternatives than its Energy Cost or Environmental Impact scores do (0.086 average swing against 0.027 and 0.032; Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), so MEREC assigns Comfort most of the weight regardless of the design's a priori 0.200.

A second check varies the value-function curvature rather than the weights: recomputing the reference scores with the logarithmic shape parameter $\alpha$ swept over $\{1.0,\,1.2,\,1.5,\,2.0\}$ (1.0 is linear) at the baseline weight vector leaves the architecture scores untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:alpha_sweep} shows that the ordering $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is invariant across all four $\alpha$ values for every model. Within each architecture, the four models' $\tau$ values are nearly flat across the sweep, confirming that the curvature parameter has negligible effect on the ranking comparison.

The sensitivity analysis reweights both sides of the comparison: under each weight vector, both the reference weighted scores and the architecture weighted scores are recomputed from that same vector. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s per-criterion scores originate from the reference calculator modules, so the two reweighted vectors are near-identical, and the comparison is not proper evidence for any kind of weight-related conclusions. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ against $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is the comparison this analysis can speak to, since both architectures' criterion scores differ substantively from the reference.

\subsection{RAG Ablation}
\label{sec:rag-ablation}
\begin{figure*}[htbp]
	\centering
	\plotRagAblation
	\caption{RAG ablation results: Kendall's $\tau$ by configuration and model on the full 90-scenario RAG dataset. Configurations are ordered by overall $\tau$.}

The RAG ablation (design in Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation-methods}, full values in Supplementary Material S2) yielded three findings. Its offline \texttt{nearest-neighbor} configuration retrieves the three most similar scenarios from within the 90-scenario RAG dataset under a leave-one-out protocol and directly assigns the similarity-weighted average of their per-criterion scores, with no LLM call. It gives $\tau = 0.001$ (95\% CI: $[-0.172,\, 0.175]$), indistinguishable from random ranking. Exemplar content matters in every model; retrieval depth matters in one. Figure~\ref{fig:rag_ablation} shows that removing scores drops $\tau$ substantially for every model, while removing hidden engineering parameters moves $\tau$ far less and in both directions.

Only DeepSeek and Gemini carry a Kendall's $\tau$ omnibus that survives the 16-test Friedman family, and within those two the top cluster separates for DeepSeek alone: its $k=1$ configuration scores below the $k=3$ setting at $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.003$ (Cliff's $\delta = 0.247$, small), whereas Gemini's six top-cluster pairs all sit at $p_{\text{Holm}} \geq 0.35$. Removing exemplar scores or drawing random exemplars also degrades score accuracy in every model, with MAE roughly half again the top-cluster level (full MAE figures in Supplementary Material S2). $k=1$ remains the recommended production setting on cost grounds for three of the four models, whose $k{=}1$ minus $k{=}3$ Kendall's $\tau$ CI contains zero: Gemini $[-0.163,\,0.142]$, GPT-OSS $[-0.128,\,0.228]$, and Qwen $[-0.047,\,0.326]$. DeepSeek is the exception, whose difference of $-0.32$ carries a CI ($[-0.471,\,-0.160]$) that excludes zero; an interval computed over the four models pooled contains zero ($[-0.120,\,0.052]$), so the pooled interval conceals this per-model reversal. $k=1$ is therefore recommended subject to a per-model check that retrieval depth does not degrade that model's ranking.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity}
\label{sec:prompt-ablation}

As found by Sclar et al.~\cite{sclar2024promptsensitivity}, prompt style can have a substantial effect on LLM output. To separate prompt and architecture effects, we re-ran both architectures under three controlled prompt perturbations across four models: removing the anchors from the scoring instructions (the unanchored variant), adding an explicit chain-of-thought scaffold before the scored response (the reasoning-scaffold variant), and rescaling the response range from 0--1 to 0--10 (the rescaled-response variant). The full matrix comprises 28 architecture--model--variant cells, 156 runs, and 30{,}420 scenario-level observations. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ has three variants, not four, because its shipped prompt has no anchors for the unanchored variant to remove. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is not included in this ablation: its single extraction call has no scoring anchors, chain-of-thought scaffold, or response scale for the three perturbations to act on (Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}).
The full per-model, per-variant results table appears in Supplementary Material S2: within each model, every $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ configuration remains above every $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ configuration on Kendall's $\tau$, so no prompt perturbation reverses the architecture ordering for a fixed model. The architecture ordering survives every perturbation when architecture and model are held together, though a weaker model running the stronger architecture can still trail a stronger model running the weaker one ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ with Qwen falls to $\tau = 0.102$ under the rescaled-response variant, below $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ with DeepSeek at $\tau = 0.167$), the same model--architecture interaction visible in the sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}).

Among the pairwise comparisons that reach significance (protocol in Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}), the control wins every comparison it enters for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$; the few significant $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ comparisons instead reflect that architecture operating near the noise floor rather than any perturbation beating the control (full statistics in Supplementary Material S2). As in the RAG ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}), ranking is more stable under perturbation than calibration: the Friedman omnibus on Kendall's $\tau$ is not significant for any $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ model, while the Top-1 omnibus is significant for DeepSeek. Prompt wording shifts which alternative is ranked first more readily than it shifts the relative order of all three. Two caveats apply. First, the reasoning-scaffold variant interfered with both reasoning and  parsing: the scaffold moves the scored payload behind free-form reasoning text, and the harness must fall back to extracting the last balanced object in the response. That variant records the lowest success rate in the matrix (0.961 for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$/GPT-OSS, against 1.000 for the rescaled-response variant), so part of the result is extraction reliability, not reasoning quality. Second, the three perturbations were applied singly, not in combination, so these results speak to the effect of each manipulation in isolation and not to interactions between them.

Because failed scenarios are excluded from their run's metric computation, every metric reported above is conditional on successful extraction. Weighting each architecture's conditional Kendall's $\tau$ by its success rate gives a failure-inclusive figure: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ falls from 0.899 to 0.872, while $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ (0.274 to 0.274) and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ (0.093 to 0.092) are essentially unchanged because both fail far less often. The one case where this matters is GPT-OSS on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, whose 88.0\% success rate lowers its success-weighted $\tau$ from 0.897 to 0.789 and its Top-1 from 91.7\% to 84.7\%. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ ordering holds for all four models on the success-weighted metric, with the narrowest margin being GPT-OSS at 0.789 against 0.272 for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$. The per-run imputed protocol in Supplementary Material S2 confirms it: the ordering survives imputation in all four models; only GPT-OSS $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ moves materially ($\tau$ from 0.897 to 0.787, Top-1 from 91.7\% to 83.7\%, MAE from 0.052 to 0.077), driven by an average of 23.4 imputed scenarios per run. This is 12\% of its test set; DeepSeek, Gemini, and Qwen move by 0.0006, 0.0000, and 0.0032 in $\tau$, and every $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ cell moves by at most 0.006. 

GPT-OSS incurred the most extraction failures, concentrated in HVAC scenarios (as shown in Supplementary Material S1). DeepSeek, Gemini, and Qwen exhibited near-zero failure rates on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$. The most common failure mode for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ is an unparseable JSON response, while for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ every failure is an extracted parameter falling outside its physical validation bounds, again almost entirely from GPT-OSS on HVAC scenarios (full failure-mode breakdown in Supplementary Material S1).

Cross-run recovery eliminates most of these failures: when a scenario fails in one run but succeeds in another, the successful run's score is used. The ``without recovery'' values exclude scenarios where all five runs failed (equivalent to the primary per-run-then-average protocol), while ``with recovery'' uses cross-run success to score every scenario. Only one scenario (GPT-OSS on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$) failed in all five runs, so the two definitions give the same GPT-OSS $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ headline (pooled $\tau$ 0.897 and Top-1 91.7\% under both). That near-total recoverability is consistent with the picture from the per-run imputed protocol above: GPT-OSS's failures fall on different scenarios from run to run rather than on a fixed, unrecoverable set.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Alternative Ordering}
\label{sec:res-order}

Reversing the alternative order changed $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s top-1 accuracy by less than a net 9 of 195 scenarios for every model (design in Section~\ref{sec:alternative-order}): DeepSeek's reversed condition was less accurate by 8.2 scenarios on a net basis, and the other three models moved by 1.4 to 3.4 scenarios in the reversed condition's favour. None of these shifts reaches significance; DeepSeek's is the largest, and its McNemar test returns a median $p = 0.477$ across the five shipped-run comparisons, consistent with ordinary run-to-run variation rather than an ordering effect (full per-model statistics in Supplementary Material S2). Ordering changed what the models extracted, and did not detectably change what
they decided: under the permutation test of Section~\ref{sec:alternative-order}, two of four
models separated at the parameter level at the exact permutation floor of the
test ($1/1287$) while no model separated on the top-1 alternative, and against
the reference ranking the reversed and control conditions differ by less than
$0.004$ Kendall's $\tau$ with the sign of that difference inconsistent across
models, so the deterministic calculator absorbs the perturbation before it
reaches the ranking (full per-model statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

One incidental observation bears on any benchmark that compares conditions collected
at different times: for DeepSeek, run-to-run agreement on extracted parameters
was $0.42$ among the shipped runs and $0.64$ and $0.67$ among the two conditions
collected in the later session, with no manipulation distinguishing the latter
two.

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}
$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ was the strongest architecture overall, and the only one whose within-scenario score spread matched the reference calculator's (0.142--0.152 across models, against 0.145). That match corroborates Li et al.~\cite{li2026scoringbias}, who found scoring biases in LLM-as-judge pipelines; $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ sidesteps these biases by removing the LLM from the scoring role entirely. The MAE reduction relative to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ is large: in absolute terms, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s residual error is roughly \$0.20 per scenario for energy cost, and about 1.4 lbs CO$_2$ (HVAC/Appliance) or 3 gallons of water (Shower), using the reference ranges in Table~\ref{table:reference_ranges}; delegating arithmetic to a specially designed assistant is more reliable than asking the LLM to score directly. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s primary limitation is reliance on the calculator: if a criterion the calculator cannot model becomes important, such as indoor humidity for an HVAC decision, which ASHRAE 55's temperature-only comfort model does not capture, the architecture falls back on the same weaknesses as other architectures. Even then the calculator keeps scoring the criteria it does model. The model gap is also narrowest under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}): the calculator reduces sensitivity to model capability.

\texttt{Fixed\-Default} performs well only where its default happens to match the domain (Section~\ref{sec:res-baselines}). It collapses on Appliance ($\tau = 0.097$): Practicality alone recovers the reference winner in 90.8\% of Appliance scenarios, and a fixed 7\,p.m.\ run time cannot track a household's actual schedule, which Practicality measures. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ clears the bar this sets overall and on Appliance and Shower, while $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ falls below the fixed default on Kendall's $\tau$ overall for all four models, and on two of the three decision types. HVAC is the exception. There the fixed default reaches $\tau = 0.933$, above $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s pooled 0.930 and above two of the four models taken singly, because an R-15, SEER-13 default sits near the optimum for the Pennsylvania housing stock these scenarios draw on. The same domain match that sinks the fixed default on Appliance carries it on HVAC.

Extraction error rarely reaches the ranking (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}), and the parameter-source ablation (Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}) explains why: this is a property of decision structure, not of extraction accuracy. If $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ extracts a house's insulation as R-14 when the true value is R-16, the underestimate raises the projected heating loss, and with it the cost and emissions estimate, for all three setpoint alternatives under comparison; the alternative that was cheapest at the true R-16 is still cheapest at the estimated R-14, because the error moves every alternative in the same direction rather than reordering them.

That cancellation depends on the alternatives sharing an underlying, jointly-uncertain state, as they do throughout this benchmark's within-household choice sets, and it weakens for a parameter that both scales the outcome directly and is recovered from a coarse homeowner-facing proxy. Shower GPM is that parameter (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}): GPM multiplies against each alternative's own duration rather than scaling all three by a common amount, so a GPM error shifts the three duration alternatives by different absolute amounts and breaks the cancellation. Deploying $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ on decisions where alternatives do not share a common underlying parameter set, such as comparing unrelated households or services, would remove this cancellation effect, a case this benchmark does not test.

GPT-OSS and Qwen recur across this benchmark's clearest reliability failures, though not through one shared mechanism. GPT-OSS accounts for nearly all $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ extraction failures, concentrated in HVAC scenarios (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}); under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, GPT-OSS and Qwen alone return identical energy-cost and environmental scores for 93.7\% and 95.6\% of Appliance alternatives, against the reference's own 8.0\% (Table~\ref{tab:score_duplication}), so neither model separates two criteria the reference treats as distinct; and Qwen's $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ ranking accuracy is higher under random exemplar retrieval than under similarity-ranked retrieval, the reverse of every other model (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}). Both models are also the weaker pair on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ Shower, where Gemini and DeepSeek show meaningful positive correlation and GPT-OSS and Qwen remain near-random (Section~\ref{sec:res-bytype}). These failures do not reduce to a single cause (an extraction-validation failure, a criterion-discrimination failure, and a retrieval-anchoring failure are three different things), but they consistently land on the same two models, which matters for deployment: a practitioner choosing between models on the strength of one decision type or criterion should not assume that ranking transfers to another.

Entropy weights support the assumed 35/30/20/15 weighting scheme; MEREC weights instead emphasize the criteria that distinguish the alternatives at hand. Under those vectors, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ loses most of its margin over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and trails it in two of 28 model--weight-vector cells (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}; full matrix in Supplementary Material S2). Both reversals use MEREC HVAC weights, which place 0.663 on Comfort against the design's 0.200. Entropy vectors increase the weight on Energy Cost and Environmental Impact and preserve the design ordering. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s advantage over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ therefore depends on emphasizing cost and emissions, the criteria that direct scoring estimates poorly. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ remains ahead of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ under every tested vector and model.

The RAG ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:rag_ablation}) shows exemplar scores anchoring the LLM's output scale, not the specific parameters (exact R-value, GPM). Seeing scored exemplars pulls $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s within-scenario score spread back toward the reference calculator's (0.145): DeepSeek moves from 0.289 under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ to 0.163 under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, and Qwen from 0.181 to 0.173. Removing scores degrades ranking accuracy far more than removing hidden engineering parameters, and selection of exemplars drives most of the RAG contribution. That pattern reverses only for Qwen, whose Kendall's $\tau$ under random exemplar selection (0.276) exceeds its score under the curated $k=3$ exemplars (0.154; Supplementary Material S2); for that model alone, exemplars similar to the query scenario appear to anchor the output scale toward the wrong value more consistently than dissimilar ones do, though the mechanism is not established here. Having scored exemplars is more important than having full context on those exemplars.

Reversing the order in which the alternatives are listed changed what two of four models extracted: Qwen's and Gemini's parameter estimates separated by 0.054 and 0.047 respectively ($p \le 0.0008$, Holm-adjusted $p \le 0.006$), both at the test's permutation floor of $1/1287$, while DeepSeek and GPT-OSS showed no detectable separation (Section~\ref{sec:res-order}). No model's top-1 choice moved detectably ($p \ge 0.24$). The perturbation reached the LLM's parameter estimates but not the calculator's ranking output. A null result at the ranking layer alone would prove nothing, because a null there looks identical whether the calculator absorbed a real perturbation or the manipulation was too weak to move anything in the first place. Here the perturbation moved two models' estimates at near-floor significance and still left the ranking unchanged, so the calculator, not insufficient test power, explains that null result.

What the calculator absorbed here is a perturbation that shifts parameters shared by all three alternatives. A perturbation that moved one alternative's parameters relative to the others would not cancel by this mechanism. Additionally, the DeepSeek session effect (run-to-run parameter agreement 0.42 in the shipped runs vs.\ 0.64--0.67 in the later session, with no manipulation) makes the robustness claim conditional on session consistency; the permutation and control arms were collected in the same session, but the shipped runs were not. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ design should transfer to domains sharing this study's structural conditions: a vetted reference calculator, a small fixed choice set, and alternatives sharing jointly-uncertain scenario-level parameters that enter all alternatives identically. This conjecture is not tested here; the GPM analysis (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}) identifies where that structural condition weakens even within this benchmark.


\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Comparison with Prior Work}
\label{sec:comparison}

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ qualifies prior claims that internal consistency implies correct output: \citet{svoboda2024} showed GPT-4 can construct an AHP model within consistency-ratio thresholds and \citet{icaart26} found structured output improves ranking consistency in COMET-based MCDA, yet in our case $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, across multiple runs, had low-variance scores while still being far from correct (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}). Internal consistency therefore does not imply correct rankings: this miscalibration generalizes the scoring biases documented by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024fairescalers} and Li et al.~\cite{li2026scoringbias} from judge contexts to multi-criteria decision scoring. This study is also the first to benchmark that miscalibration against a deterministic reference calculator rather than against other LLM judges.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ extends Case-Based Reasoning \cite{aamodtplaza1994,minorkaucher2024} to MCDA scoring, but its gain over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ plateaus because exemplar scores anchor the output scale without correcting the model's scenario-specific arithmetic (Section~\ref{sec:res-ranking}). Rather than improving LLM arithmetic through chain-of-thought prompting \cite{wei2022cot} or tool augmentation, techniques this study did not test as numeracy fixes (the related \texttt{cot\_scaffold} prompt variant in Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation} was tested only as a wording robustness check for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s scoring prompts, and lowered JSON-parsing reliability there), $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ bypasses the numeracy problem entirely \cite{imani2023mathprompter} with a single extraction call and an existing calculator. The closest previous example is when Huang and Burgherr~\cite{huangburgherr2024} built a streamlined MCDA calculator, which still requires users to possess MCDA expertise, a barrier $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ relocates from the household to whoever designs and audits the domain calculator.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Limitations of the Study}
\label{sec:limitations}

Prompt design is a potential confound in the architecture comparison. The prompt-sensitivity ablation (Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation}) addresses this directly for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, but the concern is narrowed, not removed. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$--$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ contrast is not a clean test of retrieval alone: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s prompt gives per-decision-type worked anchors, while $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s replaces those anchors with one-line criterion directions and adds retrieved exemplars, so the two differ in criterion framing as well as in retrieval. The \texttt{no\_anchors} variant, which strips $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s anchors without adding retrieval, bounds how much of that gap the anchor wording alone could explain. An extraction prompt that recovered nothing scenario-specific would still leave $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ at the dataset-median condition's $\tau = 0.641$, above the highest $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ figure recorded under any prompt variant across all four models ($\tau = 0.325$; full per-model table in Supplementary Material S2). No perturbation of the extraction prompt can reverse the architecture ordering.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is also graded against the same calculators that it uses as part of its architecture. However, the comparison is not circular: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ uses the same deterministic calculator as the reference model, with several inputs withheld and estimated from homeowner-accessible text rather than a separate or easier scoring function. The physical validation bounds (R-value, SEER, GPM ranges) derive from ASHRAE and ENERGY STAR literature, not from pilot LLM outputs. The second author reviewed the calculator specification and validation bounds before any benchmark results existed (a pre-specification review). Neither fact makes the review independent: he is a co-author and supervisor of the project. An engineer with no connection to this project auditing a separately built calculator, used as the true reference model, would close that gap (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

\label{sec:calculator-simplifications}
Four engineering imputations in the reference calculators introduce systematic error, each adopted because the true relationship is either unpublished or cannot be evaluated on the scenario data. Mains water temperature is interpolated linearly from outdoor temperature; the published correlation \cite{burch2007} needs annual mean ambient temperature, annual ambient temperature range, and day-of-year, none of which the scenario schema carries, and reports an RMS error of approximately 4\,\textdegree{}F, comparable to the interpolation it would replace. Appliance cycle rates are fixed monthly values, because no public dataset tracks per-household utilization. The shower tank-availability factor is fixed at 0.8, standing in for a stochastic prior-use process that field metering would be needed to characterize. HVAC occupancy uses a run-time-fraction approximation, since no dataset reports continuous room-level occupancy schedules for the housing stock modeled here. Each affects all architectures equally and does not bias the comparison, but the absolute MAE and RMSE values reflect the combined error of LLM extraction and calculator imputation, so a perfect LLM extractor would still produce non-zero MAE if the reference model deviates from measured household data.

A partially incorrect formula would go further and threaten the comparison itself. Unlike the imputations, a true misspecification would inflate only $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s performance: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ could rank the alternatives correctly while the calculator wrongly penalized them, and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, scored against that same flawed calculator, would appear correct too. This is the circularity risk raised above, and the same external audit would rule it out (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

Results are specific to Pennsylvania's grid and utility rate structure: the PJM marginal emissions factors and Pennsylvania time-of-use rate tiers used throughout this study do not generalize to high-renewables grids, where lower and more variable emissions intensities would compress environmental score distributions. The architecture ordering is large enough that it would likely persist, but the absolute MAE values for environmental and energy-cost criteria would shift. The environmental criterion for shower decisions uses water consumption in gallons rather than CO$_2$ emissions, so cross-type comparisons of environmental MAE should account for this distinction; within-type ranking validity is unaffected. All shower scenarios assume electric resistance water heating; gas, heat-pump, and tankless configurations are outside the current scope.

The scenario dataset was generated by crossing categorical parameters with representative numeric draws, which ensures parameter-space coverage but does not reflect real-world joint distributions: older houses tend to have worse insulation and lower SEER ratings together, and the synthetic dataset decouples that. Ranking may therefore be easier or harder than in household populations. The comparison stays unbiased because all three architectures face the same distribution, but accuracy in real-world use may be lower.

Temperature was not ablated. Lower temperatures reduce variance but may narrow the model's output range; higher ones increase diversity but risk incoherent outputs. At temperature 0 the five runs would be identical, collapsing the repeated-measures design to a single sample and leaving run-to-run variance unestimable, so 0.3 preserves enough sampling variation for that design. The reported absolute metric values are specific to this operating point. Runs were collected on 07-05-2026, with $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ rerun on 07-21-2026.

A further limitation concerns the choice sets supplied to the architectures: each scenario presents three specific alternatives that a household might compare, whereas in practice the decision space is continuous and often unstructured. With $n=3$ alternatives, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s correlated scenario-level errors are more likely to cancel in a 3-alternative ranking than in a larger choice set. Per-scenario Kendall's $\tau$ can take only the four values $-1$, $-1/3$, $+1/3$ and $+1$, so every per-scenario $\tau$ reported in this paper is a mean over a four-level variable, and the reported $\tau$ of 0.899 is an upper bound on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy for larger choice sets. Whether alternatives near the ones tested here would rank the same way rests on a monotonicity assumption: cost and energy criteria change smoothly and predictably as an alternative changes, but comfort does not always. Extending the framework from ranking a fixed choice set to generating and ranking open-ended alternatives would test that assumption directly.

The framework assumes a single decision-maker with fixed weights (aside from practicality), while real households hold conflicting preferences; multi-occupant aggregation is left to future work (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}). Implementation cost binds harder. The three reference calculators each required extensive domain research, iterative calibration, and verification against published standards (ASHRAE, ENERGY STAR, EPA eGRID), together consuming the majority of project time. That cost does not diminish $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage, but it limits how often such an architecture is worth building (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

A tool that returns a ranked recommendation invites the household to accept it, but none of the three architectures emits a calibrated confidence estimate. A scenario the model has ranked correctly and one it has inverted arrive in the same form: the same clean top choice, with no signal that lets a homeowner tell when to trust it. The failure concentrates where accuracy is lowest: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ both fall well short of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, whose own residual error concentrates in Shower scenarios where flow rate must be inferred from a coarse label (Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction}). Any deployment of these architectures should expose the estimated parameters and the per-criterion scores alongside the ranking, so the household has something to check rather than a bare recommendation.

Two further boundaries concern who the reference model represents. The dataset fixes a narrow population: every scenario sits in one of 48 Pennsylvania municipalities on one of six utility tariffs inside the PJM footprint, and assumes an occupant free to control the thermostat, appliance run time, and water-heater configuration, so a renter who cannot change the setpoint or replace the heater, or any household outside PJM, is out of scope. The value functions are a second boundary. Each criterion's 0--1 scoring curve (Section~\ref{sec:mavt-design}) is calibrated against published standards and population-level data rather than elicited from any specific household, which is what makes the reference model reproducible and auditable, but it also means the reference ranking encodes a normative account of a reasonable household rather than this household's actual trade-offs. A homeowner who weighs comfort far above its 20\% weight, tolerates a colder shower than ASHRAE~55 predicts, or works a shift that rules out a daytime wash would be ranked against a value function calibrated for someone else. Eliciting value functions from a household's stated or revealed preferences is a natural extension (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).

The nominal criterion weights (35\% environmental, 30\% energy cost, 20\% comfort, 15\% practicality) act as fixed importance weights rather than range-sensitive swing weights. In additive MAVT models, nominal weights reflect trade-offs only when scaled by realized attribute ranges within choice sets \cite{vonnitzsch1993}, and those ranges differ sharply by decision type (Supplementary Material S2): on HVAC, Comfort averages a 0.086 within-scenario swing against 0.032 for Environmental Impact and 0.027 for Energy Cost, while on Appliance, Practicality (0.095) and Comfort (0.098) dominate the spread and Environmental Impact averages 0.008. Nominal weights therefore understate the effective influence of criteria with large within-scenario ranges.



\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}
The most immediate follow-up is external calculator review: an engineer with no connection to this project reviewing $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction schema and validation bounds would directly address the circularity concern raised in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. A second, complementary check would have a household-decision expert and an LLM independently score a sample of scenarios by hand, blind to the calculator's own output, so the reference ranking can be validated against human judgment rather than only against itself; a power calculation on the expected effect size would set the sample size needed for that comparison to be conclusive.

Three tests would probe generalization. Cross-grid replication under different emissions--cost correlation structures (e.g., ERCOT's real-time pricing or a high-nuclear European grid where marginal emissions approach zero during surplus) would address the PJM specificity noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. A domain-transfer test would apply $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ to a mature calculator outside energy (e.g., actuarial life-expectancy or portfolio Sharpe-ratio estimation). An adversarial stress test would deliberately violate the calculators' structural assumptions to identify where $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage degrades.

Longer-term extensions to the framework's scope include a neurosymbolic verification layer checking extracted outputs against known physical bounds, multi-turn preference elicitation from multiple household members to move beyond the single-decision-maker assumption (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and a randomized controlled trial comparing LLM-MCDA recommendations against unaided decisions over a sustained household deployment.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study benchmarked three strategies for integrating an LLM into an MCDA workflow, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, against a deterministic MAVT reference calculator across 195 household energy scenarios. Because the reference calculator defines the reference model, the reported metrics measure fidelity to that specified model rather than real-world decision accuracy. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ reproduces the reference most accurately, and the ordering $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ holds for every model tested. Two bounds apply: the reference calculators have not been validated against observed household behavior directly, and the engineering parameters reflect Pennsylvania's PJM grid.

The contribution is an evaluation framework and an open reference implementation for LLM-fronted MCDA in residential energy, not a new model architecture. It supplies three deterministic MAVT calculators, a 285-scenario dataset split into 195 test and 90 retrieval cases, and a harness that reports Kendall's $\tau$, Top-1, MAE and RMSE per model and per decision type; all three are public, so the comparison can be rerun under a different grid, weight vector, or model without rebuilding the reference: seven weight vectors including entropy- and MEREC-derived ones (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}), a four-value sweep of the value-function curvature parameter, three ablation suites separating retrieval depth, prompt wording, and parameter source, and an exact permutation test on alternative ordering (Section~\ref{sec:res-order}).

The general point concerns where a language model belongs in a pipeline that already has, or could have, a deterministic way to check its answer. Asking a model to read a situation and produce a judgment in one step requires it both to understand the situation and to compute the outcome, and the judgment inherits whatever scoring biases and arithmetic weaknesses the model carries into that second task. Asking the model only to translate an unstructured description into the inputs a validated calculator already knows how to score removes that second task entirely. The model doing the translating no longer has to be the strongest one available: which of the four models supplied that translation step barely moved the result, while the choice of model moved the result by a wide margin whenever a model scored the alternatives itself. The same restructuring also took away a shortcut this study relied on early: a model's answers agreed with each other across runs while nearly all of them were wrong, so run-to-run agreement was not, on its own, a usable signal of which architecture to trust. None of this is specific to household energy or to multi-criteria decisions; it should generalize to any setting where a language model is currently asked to reason its way to a number that some domain-specific calculator, however narrow, could compute instead.

That restructuring is only available where a vetted calculator exists. Where none is available, a non-LLM fixed default outranks the retrieval-augmented direct-scoring design on overall Kendall's $\tau$ for all four models, at zero LLM cost; that design beats the fixed default only on Appliance decisions, where a static schedule cannot track a household's actual timing constraints, so its case rests on the specific decision types where per-scenario reasoning matters, not on Kendall's $\tau$ overall. Whether the same decomposition pays off outside residential energy, and whether the ordering reported here holds under other grids and household populations, is future work (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}).
