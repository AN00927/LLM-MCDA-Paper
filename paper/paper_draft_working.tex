\begin{abstract}
Household multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) can guide lower-impact energy choices but requires expertise to design. We introduce an evaluation framework and open implementation for LLM-fronted MCDA based on Multi-Attribute Value Theory (MAVT), comparing direct scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$), example-guided scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$), and LLM-parameterized reference scoring ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$) across 195 HVAC, appliance, and shower scenarios and four models. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ extracts engineering parameters for deterministic reference calculators, achieving Kendall's tau of 0.880--0.923 and 89.7--93.1\% Top-1 accuracy; $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ reached 0.207--0.328 and 47.0--54.5\%; $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ 0.010--0.176 and 30.0--36.7\%. Calculator access alone is not sufficient: the same reference calculator run on median parameters instead of extracted ones reaches only $\tau=0.641$, so LLM extraction accounts for 0.26--0.28 of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage. Its tau spanned just 0.043 across a range of models with a 45$\times$ difference in cost, versus 0.166 for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$. The calculators, scenario corpus, and code are released. Accuracy denotes agreement with deterministic calculators constructed from available studies and Pennsylvania datasets.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec1}
The average U.S.\ household emits roughly 8{,}744 pounds of CO\textsubscript{2}e and consumes about 10{,}791 kWh of electricity each year \cite{epa_household2024,eia_faq_electricity2024}. Globally, residential energy consumption has grown at roughly 1\% per year since 2000, driven by increasing household sizes and comfort demands in emerging economies \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}. However, a considerable portion of this usage is adjustable, creating opportunities to reduce both household spending and environmental impact \cite{dietz2009,ohler2020}.

Tools that combine optimization, knowledge of human boundaries, and decision support could recognize those opportunities, but only if people can use them without specialized training. One such framework is Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) \cite{huang2011}. In theory it should produce the environmentally optimal choice every time, in any field. However, constructing an MCDA model requires defining alternatives, criteria, weights, value functions, and normalization rules, making it impractical both in terms of time and mental demand for routine household decisions.

Modern LLMs carry general knowledge about the energy use and carbon intensity of common products and behaviours, which is relatively quick to access. They have not, however, been tested extensively for their abilities as MCDA designers \cite{svoboda2024,icaart26}. Still, no test has measured the assistance needed to bring their designer ability to a reasonably accurate level. We address that gap by benchmarking the three architectures across three household energy decision types, holding the model and the MAVT weighting constant for each architecture.

Thus, we aimed to answer the following questions: Which LLM-assisted architecture most accurately reproduces a deterministic ground truth overall ranking for rapid household decision support? Then, how consistently does the architecture ranking hold across models of varying capability?

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit-review}

\subsection{Household Energy Decision-Making and Adoption Barriers}

Globally, residential buildings account for 18\% of CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}; in the United States, the residential sector consumed 19.2 quadrillion BTU (5,627 TWh) of delivered energy in 2025 and accounted for roughly 20\% of national energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions \cite{eia2022,goldstein2020}.

Dietz et al.\ (2009) estimated that household behavioral changes could reduce U.S.\ emissions by 123 MtC (approximately 451 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e) annually \cite{dietz2009}. At the time, this would account for roughly 8.3\% of U.S.\ energy-related CO\textsubscript{2} emissions. Scaling to the U.S.'s 4{,}904 MMmt of 2025 energy-related emissions \cite{eia_co2_2025} suggests a current savings potential of 407 MtCO\textsubscript{2}e. That potential concentrates in a few areas: HVAC systems alone account for roughly a third of global residential energy consumption \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}.

\cite{attari2010} found that consumers undervalue energy use and savings by a factor of 2.8 on average, systematically underestimating high-energy activities while overestimating low-energy ones; the ``usual setting'' for most activities rests on incorrect or arbitrary assumptions, whether this is a temperature setpoint, an appliance run time, or shower time.

Constructing MCDAs is time-consuming even for experts \cite{huang2011}; even a streamlined calculator \cite{huangburgherr2024} still requires framing alternatives, criteria, and weights, keeping the tool out of routine household use.

In households, space heating accounts for 43\% of residential energy use, followed by appliances (13\%), water heating (12\%), and air conditioning (8\%) \cite{eia_recs2020}. These four categories are tested in this study as three decision types (HVAC, appliance scheduling, and water heating via shower duration), account for the large majority of controllable residential energy use (not accounting for purchases). Behavioral plasticity---how likely people are to implement each action based on financial and social incentives, captured in \cite{dietz2009}'s Reasonably Achievable Emission Reduction (RAER) metric---determines which reductions are practically attainable. Individuals often point to curtailment rather than installing new products \cite{attari2010}, even though equipment-based actions score higher in \cite{dietz2009}'s RAER analysis because one-time, incentive-backed installations achieve high adoption. This study targets curtailment-type decisions precisely because they recur daily and receive no comparable incentive infrastructure or contractor guidance, not because any single instance outweighs an equipment replacement.

Current routines, usually heuristic choices, resolve everyday decisions quickly but grow unreliable as time pressure and information load rise \cite{bobadilla2018,hahn1992}. Time pressure and mental load lead to satisficing---selecting the first acceptable option rather than evaluating trade-offs, and status quo bias keeps households locked into habitual schedules, even when shifting usage windows saves money \cite{gill2022}. Decision assistance tools impose a workflow tax on users: introducing any kind of decision-making tool, digital or otherwise, decreases the likelihood of long-term adoption \cite{ariely2001,leclerc1995}.

Natural-language interfaces can reduce this cognitive tax. In building performance, natural-language platforms like EPlus-LLM extract building parameters to cut modeling time by 95\% \cite{jiang2024eplus}, and context-aware recommendation engines that explain the financial and environmental trade-offs behind a suggestion increase user acceptance by 20\% \cite{zharova2024}.

\subsection{LLMs as Numeric Reasoners and Scorers}

For arithmetic, LLMs "often provide incorrect answers" unless self-checked against multiple generated solution paths \cite{imani2023mathprompter}. Expecting LLMs to solely be responsible for and accurately understand how competing (and often similar) alternatives can have one clear winner alone is therefore unreliable without external grounding. However, testing a lone-LLM scorer is useful to see exactly how unreliable LLMs are without that grounding.

The most conventional method of LLM assistance is few-shot prompting, which is when the prompt includes examples similar to the solution needed. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) can do that using a database of examples by injecting external facts into an LLM's context to reduce hallucination in open-domain QA \cite{lewis2020}. An MCDA scoring task is closer to Case-Based Reasoning, which treats problem-solving as retrieving similar prior cases and reusing their recorded solutions \cite{aamodtplaza1994}. \cite{minorkaucher2024} found that retrieved example explanations used as in-context demonstrations improve LLM-generated outputs over zero-shot prompting. Providing pre-scored expert scenarios could reasonably increase accuracy.

However, both implementations have fundamental flaws; \cite{wang2024fairescalers} show that LLMs exhibit positional bias when scoring, ranking the first response higher simply because of its placement (a problem with ranking alternatives), and LLMs also have inaccurate score calibration. \cite{li2026scoringbias} document systematic scoring-bias distortions in LLM-as-a-judge pipelines that persist even in advanced models, and \cite{zhu2026cyclicjudge} trace most of the cross-run score variance on benchmarks like MT-Bench to judge identity rather than response quality. \cite{policar2025bioinformatics} show that different LLM graders carry distinct, model-specific biases. Based on this prior work, we would predict that $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ fails for one reason: the four criterion scores for all three alternatives cluster into a narrow middle band, missing the sharp, non-linear penalties in the ground-truth value functions, dragging down Kendall's tau even when scores are directionally reasonable.

An alternative to having the LLM score directly is to have it estimate the missing engineering parameters and let a deterministic calculator handle the scoring. This LLM-parameterized approach preserves the transparency of equation-based evaluation while leveraging the LLM's pattern-matching ability to infer quantitative values from natural-language context. The tradeoff is narrower applicability: a calculator must be built for each decision domain. We test this tradeoff directly through $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, which pairs single-call LLM parameter extraction with the three ground-truth calculators.

Despite extensive work on LLM numeracy, retrieval-augmented generation, and LLM-as-judge scoring, no prior study has rigorously compared these integration strategies against a deterministic reference calculator calibrated to published engineering standards for household energy decisions. We fill this gap by benchmarking three LLM-MCDA architectures---$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$---across 195 test scenarios spanning HVAC, appliance, and shower decisions, using identical MAVT weighting and model access so that the integration strategy is the sole variable under test.

\subsection{Decision Types and Scenario Corpus}
Three decision types were selected for their high behavioral plasticity and RAER: HVAC (adjusting thermostat setpoints), Appliance (time-of-use [TOU] scheduling), and Shower (duration). HVAC was prioritized because space heating and cooling account for approximately a third of global residential energy consumption and represent the fastest-growing end-use as household incomes rise \cite{GONZALEZTORRES2022112428}.

The scenario corpus has two pools. The test set contains 195 scenarios (70 HVAC, 65 Appliance, and 60 Shower); each scenario presents three alternatives scored independently by each architecture and by the ground-truth calculator. The RAG scenarios (detailed in Supplementary Material S1) contains an additional 90 scenarios (35 HVAC, 35 Appliance, and 20 Shower) that populate the retrieval index used by the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ architecture; RAG scenarios are used only in the retrieval index \cite{lewis2020}.

The scenario counts were chosen to ensure full coverage of the parameter space rather than to satisfy a statistical power calculation. Each decision type's scenario set crosses the categorical parameters (insulation tier, housing type, appliance type, flow-rate band) with representative numeric draws (outdoor temperature, square footage, household size, utility budget), with coverage of every categorical combination ensured by a combinatorial audit (detailed in Supplementary Material S1). The 90 RAG scenarios follow the same parameter distribution without excess scenarios in any combination and are kept fully disjoint from the test set, so no test scenario shares an identical parameter signature with a retrieval-index entry.

The test corpus withholds each scenario's exact engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle, occupancy flags) behind homeowner-accessible labels for every architecture, while $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$'s retrieved RAG exemplars still expose their own engineering values as worked examples (Table~\ref{tab:parameter_comparison}; Section~\ref{sec:architectures}).

The ground-truth calculators in this study were built on Pennsylvania-specific data (electricity and natural gas rates, utility structures, and PJM marginal emission factors. The models tested were made aware of this through the locations given.

\subsection{MAVT Framework \& Weighting Design}
\label{sec:mavt-design}
The Multi-Attribute Value Theory (MAVT) allows alternatives to be compared on different criteria based on the range of values that occurs for each criterion \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}.

The weights are held constant across all decision types, all architectures, and the ground-truth calculator. We did this so that any difference in score quality between decision types is because of the input accuracy and value function, not because of difference in the way each criterion weighted for the three decision types. Normally, MAVT additive form (Eq.~\ref{eq:mavt_sum}) requires preferential independence among criteria \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}. This means that criteria should be independent, meaning that the value (good or bad) of one criterion should have no effect on the value of another criterion.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mavt_sum}
s_j = \sum_{i=1}^{4} w_i \cdot v_i(x_{ij})
\end{equation}

In our dataset the energy cost and environmental impact criteria are not statistically independent: Spearman's $\rho$ between them is 0.92. This is not, however, a violation of the condition the additive form requires. Preferential independence is a property of the preference structure---that the rate at which the decision maker trades one criterion against another does not depend on the levels of the remaining criteria---and not a property of the empirical correlation between attribute values in a particular set of alternatives \cite{keeneyraiffa1976,dyer1979}. A household willing to accept a given increment of emissions for a given saving in cost at 20\,$^\circ$F would accept the same trade at 90\,$^\circ$F, so the additive form remains admissible at $\rho = 0.92$.

The correlation does, however, raise a distinct and legitimate concern;redundancy, or double counting, -which arises if the two criteria are operationally the same quantity under different names, causing their combined 65\% weight to be applied twice to a single underlying attribute \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}. The extent to which this occurs differs sharply by decision type. Because ranking is performed within a scenario, the relevant quantity is the within-scenario association between the two criteria across the three alternatives.

For the Appliance decision type the two criteria genuinely separate. The price period is set by the utility's time-of-use window (for PECO, 2--6\,p.m.\ on weekdays \cite{peco2021}) while the emissions period is set by the PJM system-wide peak of 7\,a.m.--11\,p.m., so a cycle run at 8\,a.m.\ is off-peak for cost and on-peak for emissions. The magnitudes differ as sharply as the windows (the peak-to-off-peak rate and emissions differentials are reported in Supplementary Material S1).

For HVAC and Shower they do not separate. In both, the parameters that distinguish the emissions or water quantity from the cost quantity---the occupancy-resolved emissions factor for HVAC, the flow rate and heater setpoint for Shower---are scenario-level and therefore identical across the three alternatives, so within any one scenario the environmental quantity is a fixed scalar multiple of the cost quantity. The within-scenario Spearman correlation is 1.000 for 101 of the 105 HVAC scenarios (test and RAG corpora combined; the four exceptions have a constant raw cost across the three alternatives, so $\rho$ is undefined) and for all 80 Shower scenarios, and after normalization the two criterion scores differ by a mean of only 0.006 (HVAC) and 0.053 (Shower) on the 0--1 scale. For these two decision types the four-criterion specification therefore operates as a three-criterion one, with an effective weight of 0.65 on a single cost/emissions dimension. This is a property of the specification that is a byproduct of available data for the ground truth. The two would diverge on a grid whose marginal emissions intensity varies independently of retail price, which the PJM footprint studied here does not (Section~\ref{sec:calculator-simplifications}).

In our study, the benchmark harness uses the following weight configuration: Environmental Impact (35\%), Energy Cost (30\%), Comfort (20\%), and Practicality (15\%), as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:item3}. The vector is an input held constant across architectures and decision types so that the comparison is controlled, and results are reported across multiple weight configurations, including the baseline, equal weights, and the entropy- and MEREC-derived objective weight vectors in the weight robustness (Appendix~\ref{app:weights}).

The Environmental Impact weight of 0.35 reflects the sizable share of national CO\textsubscript{2} emissions attributable to the residential sector \cite{dietz2009} and Value--belief--norm theory's finding that environmental orientation is the most consistent normative driver across pro-environmental household behaviours \cite{stern1999,steg2005}. Additionally, tests based on entrophy-based normalization of the scenario distributions independently gives high weight to this criterion \cite{roszkowska2026}, though these weights are computed on post-value-function scores and are therefore descriptive rather than normative (full computation in Appendix~\ref{app:weights}).

Energy Cost is weighted at 0.30, just below environmental impact, because cost is the strongest predictor of consumer adoption---energy-label cost savings outperform actual cost and emissions information in shaping behavior \cite{newell2014}---and households respond to billing-rate signals across all three decision types: thermostat setpoints determine electricity draw \cite{managementscience_pricing2023}, appliance scheduling shifts cost sharply with time-of-use rates (peak-to-off-peak savings detailed in Supplementary Material S1), and shower energy cost is heavily affected by the visibility bias documented by Attari et al. \cite{attari2010,reu2016,harrispoll2024}.

Comfort is weighted at 0.20, reflecting its role as the second driver of HVAC behavior \cite{aqilah2022,karjalainen2007} via ASHRAE\,55 setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020,fanger1970,vanhoof2008}. For appliances, it follows demand-response acceptance research on tolerance for scheduling delay \cite{paetz2012,waseem2020}, and for showers it peaks near the REU2016 average duration and penalizes heater setpoints below CDC Legionella thresholds \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026}.

Practicality, weighted at 0.15, captures the feasibility and sustainability of an alternative rather than a household's unconstrained preference, since low adoption is a longer term effect of friction \cite{ariely2001,leclerc1995,gardner2020,mergelsberg2020}; it penalizes rather than eliminates infeasible options since most options would become infeasible if they were repeated for extended periods, which is not gaurenteed. A 20-minute shower in a four-person household with a 50-gallon tank is the clearest case: at 2.5\,GPM the four showers together draw about 155 gallons of hot water against the roughly 40 gallons the tank can usefully supply, so the household runs out. The Shower calculator registers this by subtracting a fixed capacity penalty from practicality rather than removing the alternative from the choice set.

By requiring a score or parameter estimate for each of the four criteria, the structured framework prevents the LLM from arbitrarily selecting an alternative based on a single dominant attribute, a known problem with LLM judgment \cite{wang2024fairescalers}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:norm_x}
\tilde{x} =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for increasing criteria}\\[4mm]
\dfrac{x_{\max} - x}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}} & \text{for decreasing criteria}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Energy cost and environmental impact enter as raw physical quantities (dollars, and lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2} or gallons of water); comfort and practicality are emitted by the calculators already on a 0--1 utility scale, so for those two $x$ is that score rather than a physical measurement. Each criterion's raw quantity is first converted to a direction-adjusted normalized value $\tilde{x}$, so that higher values are always better:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:linear_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \tilde{x}
\end{equation}

The environmental and energy cost functions use the linear value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:linear_vf}) because equal absolute changes in these quantities represent equal improvements: a 1\,lb\,CO\textsubscript{2} reduction is equally valuable regardless of the baseline, and a \$0.10 saving carries the same decision weight at any cost level relative to the household's budget range \cite{dyer1979,keeneyraiffa1976}. Framing environmental impact in absolute physical units (lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2}) means the same logic is applicable \cite{keeneyraiffa1976}.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:log_vf}
v_i(\tilde{x}) = \frac{\ln\!\left(1 + \alpha \cdot \tilde{x}\right)}{\ln(1 + \alpha)}
\end{equation}

The comfort and practicality criteria use the logarithmic value function (Eq.~\ref{eq:log_vf}) because both exhibit diminishing marginal returns: initial improvements from an uncomfortable or impractical baseline yield disproportionately large perceived gains, while incremental improvements near the optimum matter progressively less. For comfort, moving from a temperature far outside the ASHRAE~55 band toward the neutral setpoint produces large subjective gains, but further refinement within an already acceptable range has little additional effect \cite{ashrae55_2020,fanger1970,vanhoof2008}. For practicality, adopting a behavior that fits within budget and routine constraints is a large step; further reducing friction beyond an already feasible option adds minimal value \cite{ariely2001,leclerc1995}. The shape parameter $\alpha = 1.5$ for comfort and $\alpha = 1.2$ for practicality were set a priori to introduce mild concavity and partially adjusted to ensure a lack of clamping (e.g. values that jump out of the 0-1 range). No elicitation was performed (e.g., midvalue splitting), and the values are not derived from data. Moderate gains (e.g., moving from the 25th to the 50th percentile of the criterion range) receive meaningful credit without over-rewarding marginal further improvements, while keeping the function close enough to linear that the difference in weights remains the dominant driver of the final MAVT score. Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity} reports the architecture ordering under all four $\alpha$ values; $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is invariant across $\alpha \in \{1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 2.0\}$.

For HVAC, cost percentiles reflect active-conditioning alternatives. A setpoint near outdoor temperature produces zero decision-period load and collapses to \$0, which makes the lower bound go to zero. Cost normalization uses a bundled Pennsylvania retail rate of \$0.19/kWh. EIA reports a lower state average of about \$0.177/kWh, but that figure is an average revenue per kWh over all residential customers in the state, including those on legacy supply contracts. The rate used here is instead the all-in amount a household on default service pays at the margin: generation supply plus transmission, distribution, and the customer-assistance and energy-efficiency riders that appear as separate line items on the bill, priced against the default-service rates of the six Pennsylvania utilities modelled here \cite{paupc2025,eia_faq_electricity2024}. Excluding zero-load points, the realized 8-hour cost distribution at this rate spans \$0.38--\$3.29. An Off option receives top cost score via decreasing-value normalization. These endpoints match residential HVAC efficiency benchmarks \cite{synapse2013,parker2018,huyen2019}.

Using this kWh distribution with PJM marginal emissions factors (0.976 off-peak, 1.041 peak lbs CO\textsubscript{2}/kWh) yields HVAC environmental bounds of 1.96--18.04 lbs CO\textsubscript{2} \cite{pjm2022emissions}.


For appliances, the realized per-cycle cost distribution (kWh/cycle multiplied by the resolved time-of-use rate across the six PA utilities) spans \$0.025--\$0.71, and the realized per-cycle emissions distribution (kWh/cycle through the PJM marginal factor at run time) spans 0.288--3.643 lbs CO\textsubscript{2}. The underlying per-cycle energy endpoints remain consistent with the ENERGY STAR certified-products distribution (efficient HE washers near 0.25\,kWh) and include the older non-certified electric resistance dryers \cite{energystar_products,chenyu2018,hustvedt2013,neep2015,porras2020}.

For showers, the realized per-shower cost distribution spans \$0.14--\$1.14. Shower energy follows the mixing-fraction physics targeting a fixed 105\,\textdegree{}F delivery temperature, so it depends only on the rise from the mains inlet (seasonal inlet temperatures from NREL hot-water modeling \cite{hendron2008}) to that target and is independent of heater setpoint for setpoints at or above the 105\,\textdegree{}F delivery target, which all corpus setpoints (105--140\,\textdegree{}F) satisfy. 

Shower environmental impact is defined as water volume (gallons); the realized distribution of flow rate multiplied by duration spans 6.0--45.0 gallons at the 5th--95th percentile, with flow-rate and duration benchmarks from EPA WaterSense Appendix~B \cite{epawatersense}, REU2016 \cite{reu2016}, and The Harris Poll \cite{harrispoll2024}. Water volume was chosen because it was the major environmental footprint of showering, and showers account for a major share of indoor residential water use. However, this means that models are tested on slightly different grdounds between decision types.

The endpoints above were obtained by running every corpus scenario through its decision type's calculator, test and retrieval scenarios alike, pooling the raw quantity across all three alternatives of every scenario of that type, and taking the 5th and 95th percentiles of the pooled distribution. For HVAC, alternatives carrying zero conditioning load are dropped from the pool, so the normalization floor is set by the cheapest actively conditioning option rather than by a degenerate zero. The one endpoint not read off a realized distribution is the HVAC environmental bound, which is converted from the HVAC energy percentile envelope through the PJM factors as described above, so that the cost and emissions scales for that decision type stay physically consistent with each other. The endpoints are then held as constants inside each calculator's value function rather than recomputed at run time, so that a later change to the scenario corpus cannot move the normalization without the change being made explicitly.

\subsection{Ground Truth Calculators}
\label{sec:gt-calculators}
Each ground-truth calculator takes a scenario with three alternatives and returns four scores per alternative plus the raw physical quantities for environmental impact and energy cost before value-function transformation (not used in any architecture scoring). Energy cost is in dollars per 8-hour decision period (HVAC), per cycle (Appliance), or per shower (Shower); monthly estimates are computed only for the budget penalty; Environmental Impact is in lbs\,CO\textsubscript{2} for HVAC and Appliance decisions and in gallons of water for Shower decisions.

For HVAC and Appliance decisions, environmental impact uses PJM marginal emissions factors:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \mathrm{CO_2} = E_{\mathrm{kWh}} \cdot \varepsilon(t), \\[4pt]
  \varepsilon(t) = \begin{cases}
    1.041 \text{ lbs/kWh} & \text{peak (7am--11pm)} \\
    0.976 \text{ lbs/kWh} & \text{off-peak}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
  \label{eq:emissions}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $E_{\mathrm{kWh}}$ is the decision-period energy use and $\varepsilon(t)$ is the PJM marginal emissions factor at run time $t$ \cite{pjm2022emissions}. Marginal factors are used rather than averages because shifting a residential load displaces or adds the generator at the margin.
All three calculators apply an identical four-tier budget penalty $P(u)$ multiplicatively to the energy-cost value-function output, where $u = C_{\mathrm{monthly}} / B$ is monthly cost utilization relative to the household's stated budget $B$:
\begin{equation}
  P(u) = \begin{cases}
    1.0                          & u < 0.80                   \\[4pt]
    1 - 2.5\,(u - 0.80)         & 0.80 \le u < 1.0           \\[4pt]
    0.5\,e^{-3(u - 1.0)}        & 1.0 \phantom{0}\le u < 1.5 \\[4pt]
    0                            & u \ge 1.5
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:budget_penalty}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $P(u)$ is a unitless multiplicative penalty on the (post value-function) energy-cost score, $u$ is monthly budget utilization, $C_{\mathrm{monthly}}$ is estimated monthly energy/water-related cost for the decision type, $B$ is the household-stated monthly budget for utilities, and $e$ is Euler's number.
\noindent The no-penalty zone ($u < 0.80$) reflects mental budget safety margins \cite{thaler1999}; the linear decline approaching the limit follows \cite{heath1996}'s budget self-control model; exponential loss aversion for overruns follows \cite{prelec1998} and \cite{heutel2017}; and elimination beyond 150\% reflects financial infeasibility \cite{gathergood2012}. Applying the penalty multiplicatively ensures extremely cheap or expensive alternatives cannot completely sway ranking on energy cost alone.

 For HVAC decisions, $C_{\mathrm{monthly}} = 90\,C_{\mathrm{8h}}$, where $C_{\mathrm{8h}}$ is the energy cost for one 8-hour conditioning period and 90 is three such periods per day across 30 days. The Appliance and Shower monthly-cost conversions follow the same pattern (full equations in Supplementary Material S1).

\subsubsection{HVAC Calculator}
Thermal load is a four-component ASHRAE-style balance (conductive, internal-gain, solar, and infiltration, with housing-type envelope multipliers and an always-on standby baseline \cite{accamanualj,ashrae_hof2017,polly2011,ashrae62_1_1989}), divided by a SEER-derived effective EER \cite{ahri2008}, then scaled by an occupancy multiplier (1.0 fully occupied, 0.75 overnight, and a linear day-away scaling for unoccupied daytime periods \cite{houde2021}) to give decision-period energy use over the 8-hour window:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  Q_{\mathrm{cond}} &= \tfrac{1}{R}\,m_h A_f\,\Delta T, &
  Q_{\mathrm{inf}}  &= 1.08\,\tfrac{A_f h_c\,\mathrm{ACH}}{60}\,\Delta T, \\[2pt]
  Q_{\mathrm{int}}  &= 400\,n + A_f + 800, &
  Q_{\mathrm{sol}}  &= 20\,(0.15\,A_f),
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:hvac_load}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  Q = \max\!\left(0,\;
  \begin{cases}
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} + Q_{\mathrm{int}} + Q_{\mathrm{sol}} & \text{cooling} \\[3pt]
    Q_{\mathrm{cond}} + Q_{\mathrm{inf}} - Q_{\mathrm{int}}                    & \text{heating}
  \end{cases}\right)
  \label{eq:hvac_load_total}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{Q}{1000\,\mathrm{EER}}\,H\,r(o),
  \qquad
  \mathrm{EER} = 1.12\,S - 0.02\,S^{2}
  \label{eq:hvac_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $R$ is the envelope R-value, $A_f$ the conditioned floor area (ft\textsuperscript{2}), $m_h$ the housing-type envelope multiplier (1.2 apartment and condo, 1.5 townhouse and rowhouse, 1.6 twin, 1.7 single-family), $\Delta T$ the indoor--outdoor temperature difference, $h_c$ the ceiling height, $\mathrm{ACH}=0.35$ the infiltration air-change rate, $n$ the household size, $H=8$ the decision-period length in hours, $S$ the rated SEER, and $r(o)$ the occupancy multiplier. Solar gain enters only in cooling season, and internal gain reverses sign in heating season because occupant and equipment heat offsets the load the system must supply. The 800\,BTU/hr standby term keeps a zero-$\Delta T$ setpoint from producing exactly zero load. Environmental impact applies an occupancy-resolved PJM marginal emissions factor to that energy use, using the peak/off-peak split of Eq.~\ref{eq:emissions} . Age- and maintenance-driven efficiency degradation is not applied to this energy path; it appears instead as a reliability factor on the practicality score, so the energy score reflects the setpoint choice rather than the system's condition and degredation.

Comfort uses a tent function anchored to ASHRAE\,55 PMV-optimal setpoints \cite{ashrae55_2020,vanhoof2008,fanger1970} ($76\,^\circ$F in cooling mode, $70\,^\circ$F in heating mode), with an additional penalty term for household sizes $n > 3$

Practicality utility follows a piecewise value function of setpoint extremity, indoor--outdoor $\Delta T$, and equipment age. This is the first of five comfort and practicality utilities defined this way across the three calculators. The extremity slopes steepen on the least-tolerable directions, the outdoor--indoor $\Delta T$ feasibility factor is used to show operation near system limits, and the degradation change which takes into account that an older or poorly maintained unit is a less practical choice to rely on.

An ``Off'' alternative (no active conditioning) is scored with zero decision-period energy, cost, and emissions; its comfort and practicality are instead evaluated against the free-float indoor temperature the building drifts to with the system off. These are outdoor $+5$\,\textdegree{}F in cooling season and $+10$\,\textdegree{}F in heating season, reflecting solar and internal gains that hold a closed, occupied house above outdoor air temperature \cite{ashrae55_2020,accamanualj,ashrae_hof2017}. In the worked example of Supplementary Material S1, these value functions make $76\,^\circ$F the recommended setpoint because it coincides with the ASHRAE\,55 comfort optimum by design, with $80\,^\circ$F ranking second (its comfort penalty four degrees above the optimum outweighs its energy savings under the given weight vector) and $72\,^\circ$F last, since it imposes the highest cost, exceeds the \$200 monthly budget and triggers the exponential tier of the budget penalty $P$, and offers no comfort advantage over $76\,^\circ$F.

Full equations, coefficients, and citations for this calculator are in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsubsection{Appliance Calculator}
Energy cost per cycle is kWh/cycle multiplied by the location- and time-specific TOU rate, resolved to one of six Pennsylvania utilities (PECO, PPL, West Penn, Penelec, MetEd, Duquesne) \cite{peco2021,ppl2025,firstenergy2025,firstenergy2026,duquesne2026} by mapping household location to a serving utility and then that utility's peak/off-peak window. The PJM emissions window (7am--11pm) applies to environmental impact regardless of the utility's billing window, because emissions follow the regional grid.

The calculator does not model weekend, holiday, or seasonal peak-window shifts, using weekday summer TOU rates year-round.

Comfort utility is parameterized by delay, appliance type, housing type, household size, and run-time hour (late-night runs, 22:00--07:00, incur a noise penalty); practicality utility by delay, run-time hour, and household coordination difficulty.

The late-night noise penalty scales with housing type: apartment and condo units receive the largest multiplier ($\kappa=1.5$) because shared walls transmit vibrations directly, townhouse and rowhouse an intermediate ($\kappa=1.2$), and isolated single-family homes the smallest ($\kappa=0.8$), and it is joined by a household-size penalty reflecting the empirical finding that multi-person households experience disproportionate annoyance from delayed appliance cycles \cite{waseem2020}. The penalty fires when the appliance's rated sound level exceeds 45\,dBA, which penalizes washers and dryers (74 and 65\,dBA) and leaves a dishwasher, rated at 45\,dBA, exactly at the threshold and therefore unpenalized. The threshold is a categorical design choice, not an indoor limit: the EPA/WHO 35\,dBA figure applies to sound received in a sleeping space \cite{epa1974noise}, whereas the calculator compares rated source levels and applies no attenuation model, so the two quantities are not directly comparable. Timing complexity adds a practicality penalty at run-time hours whose low-price zones sit at the brink of the day, perceived as too early or too late to act on \cite{paetz2012}.

For budget utilization in \eqref{eq:budget_penalty}, the appliance calculator converts per-cycle cost to a monthly estimate using appliance-specific cycles-per-month estimates, from 215, 300, and 283 cycles/year for dishwashers, washers, and dryers respectively \cite{energystar_dishwasher_criteria2023,energystar_clotheswasher,doe_dryer_tp_2011}.

Full equations, coefficients, and citations for this calculator are in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsubsection{Shower Calculator}
Mains inlet temperature is applied from outdoor temperature between 45\,\textdegree{}F (winter) and 65\,\textdegree{}F (summer) \cite{hendron2008,backman2013,maguire2013}, setting the fraction of flow requiring heating to reach a $105\,^\circ$F delivery target \cite{zhang2023}; shower energy and environmental impact (water volume) follow from that mixing fraction, flow rate, and duration, and energy cost applies a flat electricity rate to that energy:
\begin{equation}
  f = \min\!\left(1,\;\max\!\left(0,\;\frac{T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}{T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)\right),
  \qquad
  E_{\mathrm{kWh}} = \frac{f\,G\,\rho\,(T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}})\,t}{3412\,\eta}
  \label{eq:shower_energy}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $f$ is the hot-water mixing fraction, $T_{\mathrm{d}} = 105\,^\circ$F the delivery target, $T_{\mathrm{in}}$ the mains inlet temperature, $T_{\mathrm{tank}}$ the water-heater setpoint, $G$ the flow rate in GPM, $\rho = 8.33$\,lbs/gallon, $t$ the duration in minutes, 3412 the BTU-per-kWh conversion, and $\eta = 0.92$ the electric water-heater efficiency. Because $f$ and the temperature rise $T_{\mathrm{tank}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$ move inversely, their product reduces to $T_{\mathrm{d}} - T_{\mathrm{in}}$, which is why shower energy depends on the inlet temperature but not on the heater setpoint for any setpoint at or above the delivery target.

Comfort takes into account duration, heater setpoint, household contention, and outdoor temperature, with a fixed penalty outside the 110--130\,\textdegree{}F heater band for Legionella risk below it and scald risk above it \cite{cdc2026}. Practicality, on the other hand, takes into account duration and tank-capacity feasibility, where the hot water required is the per-shower draw multiplied by the number of occupants, so household size enters practicality through the capacity test rather than as a separate term. The comfort-optimal duration follows the REU2016 7.8-minute average above 77\,\textdegree{}F and reaches about 10.3 minutes at the winter cap (10\% per 10.8\,\textdegree{}F drop). This cold-weather elasticity is a modeling assumption supported only in direction by the literature \cite{wong2022,reu2016}, rather than a directly reported coefficient, and the cap as a cross-population extrapolation from a southern-Spain sample; warm-weather scoring is unchanged.

For budget utilization in the budget penalty equation, shower monthly cost is estimated from per-shower cost using an average showers-per-person-per-day rate of 0.9 \cite{reu2016}.

The anchor points that set the band boundaries are sourced (the 7.8-minute REU2016 average, the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold, the CDC Legionella range, and the ASHRAE\,55 comfort band \cite{reu2016,harrispoll2024,cdc2026,ashrae55_2020}). The penalty magnitudes and slopes between those boundaries are design choices for which the literature fixes a direction but not a size. Storage-tank studies report usable capacity ratios of 70--85\% of nominal volume across inlet configurations \cite{abdidin2024}, and the DOE first-hour rating procedure ends a draw once outlet temperature falls 15\,\textdegree{}F below its maximum \cite{shen2021}, which places the 0.80 usable-tank fraction inside a measured range without pinning it there. Storage simulations show outlet temperature dropping below a usable shower level once a long draw exhausts the hot layer \cite{marini2017}, which supports treating capacity exhaustion as a discrete failure rather than a graded decline but says nothing about the 3.0 magnitude. Metered shower durations are right-skewed with a thin upper tail \cite{wilkes2005,mazzoni2022}, which supports a practicality curve that steepens past 15 minutes without identifying its slope. Occupancy-scaled hot-water models raise both event probability and simultaneity with each added occupant \cite{rouleau2019,kenway2016}, but no study converts shower queuing into a per-minute satisfaction penalty, so the 0.5 per-minute contention penalty above four occupants carries a supported sign and an unsupported magnitude.

The shower practicality curve measures likelihood of adoption rather than momentary preference, so it is not centered on the 7.8-minute comfort optimum. Beyond the 15-minute Harris Poll threshold the curve declines conservatively: 33\% of adults self-report showers longer than 15\,min \cite{harrispoll2024}, while the metered REU2016 data implies a much lower true rate \cite{reu2016}. The duration curve is not the only route by which a long shower loses practicality: because the capacity test scales required hot water by occupancy, a duration that is unremarkable for one person can exhaust the tank for four, and the curve and the capacity penalty then compound.

Full equations, coefficients, and citations for this calculator are in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsection{Architecture Implementations}
\label{sec:architectures}
All three architectures call the same model per test via OpenRouter at temperature 0.3, with model held constant within each run. The study evaluates four models spanning a range of capability, reasoning support, and cost: GPT-OSS-20B (reasoning, low effort), Qwen 3.5 9B (non-reasoning), DeepSeek V4 Flash (hybrid reasoning), and Gemini 3.5 Flash (reasoning, minimal effort), each set to its lowest available reasoning tier (OpenRouter model identifiers and routing variant in Supplementary Material S1). Alternative order in the prompt was fixed in scenario-sheet order across all shipped runs; Section~\ref{sec:alternative-order} reports the test of whether that choice matters. Ties in the MAVT aggregate were broken deterministically by the priority order environmental, energy cost, comfort, practicality (full tie-rate breakdown by model and architecture in Supplementary Material S1).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$}{A_D} (Direct Prompting)}
The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ architecture issues one API call per alternative (three calls per scenario) using a calibrated system prompt that combines role-prompting \cite{shanahan2023}, structured-output constraints \cite{wei2022cot}, and explicit bias-mitigation language drawn from the NIST framework for identifying and managing AI bias \cite{nistsp1270}. Beyond the scenario description, the prompt supplies no external context. 
The system prompt establishes the analyst role and, for each of the three decision types, gives each of the four criteria a short anchor phrase describing what a good and a poor score look like on that criterion. It closes with a strict JSON-only output instruction and a reminder to distinguish between criteria, consistent with the bias-mitigation language cited above (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1).
The user prompt provides the scenario context and asks the model to score one alternative at a time 

The anchors are deliberately qualitative rather than numeric. For HVAC and Shower, each criterion is described by a good/moderate/poor triple stated in terms of the physical situation. The Appliance anchors reference the off-peak and peak rate periods and the overnight minimum of the grid-emissions profile, coarser domain information than the HVAC and Shower anchors carry. Supplying no anchors at all leaves the 0--1 scale undefined, which is the condition under which LLM scorers exhibit the strongest scoring bias and compress their outputs into a narrow band \cite{li2026scoringbias,nistsp1270}. The verbal triple provides some assistance the direction and the endpoints of each scale without fixing its calibration. The technique was inspired from the triangular fuzzy numbers used to encode linguistic judgements in fuzzy MCDA \cite{zadeh1965,vanlaarhoven1983,chen2000}, which was not implemented due to its resemblance to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$. Neither prompt states any Pennsylvania-specific quantity: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is given the location and must supply the PECO time-of-use tiers and the PJM marginal emissions factors from its own knowledge.

A full worked example tracing $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s per-alternative scoring and MAVT aggregation through one HVAC scenario is in Supplementary Material S1.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$}{A_E} (Example-Guided LLM Scoring)}
The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ architecture retrieves the single most similar scenario ($k=1$) from a ChromaDB vector store before scoring, using the sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embedding model. The retrieval index is built once from the 90 RAG-only scenarios described in Section~3.1; test scenarios are used only as query vectors and the index stores only RAG scenarios, not tested scenarios \cite{lewis2020}. Like $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ uses three API calls per scenario, one per alternative.

The retrieved exemplar is rendered as a full worked example: its household-reported parameters, its engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle, tank size, heater setpoint, etc.), and each of its alternatives with the four ground-truth criterion scores plus the MAVT aggregate and rank. The exemplar's engineering values are shown so the model can see how those values map to scores. The model is instructed to use the exemplar as reference but to score the target scenario independently.

The system prompt keeps $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s analyst role, JSON-only output format, and criteria-distinction instruction, but replaces the twelve per-decision-type anchor descriptions with four one-line statements of each criterion's direction: lower cost, lower emissions, higher comfort, easier implementation. The retrieved exemplar supplies scoring guidance in the anchors' place (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1).

The user prompt presents the target scenario, the alternative to score, and the retrieved exemplar---with household-reported parameters, engineering values, and the four ground-truth scores plus MAVT aggregate and rank---instructing the model to ``consider how this specific alternative performs given the scenario context.''

The retrieval count $k=1$ was selected based on a development-set ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation-methods}): no retrieval count differed detectably at the power available in three of the four models, whose percentile bootstrap 95\% CIs on the $k{=}1$ minus $k{=}3$ Kendall's $\tau$ difference all contain zero (Gemini $[-0.163, 0.142]$, GPT-OSS $[-0.128, 0.228]$, Qwen $[-0.047, 0.326]$), DeepSeek is the exception, with $k{=}1$ sitting 0.316 below $k{=}3$ (CI $[-0.471, -0.161]$). $k=1$ was chosen as the production setting to minimize token and API cost as the cheapest configuration not shown to be worse (full ablation results in Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}).

Embedding-space coverage was measured using a nearest-neighbour versus random-draw L2 distances between Test scenario and corpus embeddings (full statistics in Supplementary Material S1).

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$}{A_H} (LLM-Parameterized Reference Scoring)}
The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ architecture issues a single API call per scenario for structured parameter extraction. Known household-reported scenario fields (outdoor temperature, square footage, insulation tier, household size, housing type, utility budget, and location) are passed directly to the calculator without LLM involvement, and the three alternatives are taken verbatim from the scenario sheet. The LLM is asked only to estimate the genuinely withheld engineering parameters that are absent from the household-reported description. For HVAC, the wall R-value, SEER rating, and HVAC age, with the occupancy context available from the question text. For Appliance, the kWh/cycle---which comes from the appliance type---and current baseline time, which is also inferrable from the question text. For Shower, the numeric GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint.

The extracted JSON is checked to make sure it is not impossible before execution. A validation script checks extracted parameters against physical limits: HVAC wall R-value (1.0--60.0), SEER (6.0--30.0), equipment age (0.0--60.0 years); appliance energy (0.05--10.0 kWh/cycle); and shower flow rate (0.5--8.0 GPM), tank capacity (10.0--120.0 gallons), and water heater setpoint (80.0--160.0\,$^\circ\text{F}$). If an LLM predicts an out-of-range value or returns non-numeric text, the script marks the extraction as a failure. Validated parameters are merged with the known fields and passed to the decision type's corresponding ground-truth calculator.

The extraction prompt uses decomposed prompting \cite{khot2023} to separate natural-language understanding from quantitative computation: unlike $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$, it does not ask the LLM to score alternatives, only to extract the withheld engineering parameters into a fixed per-decision-type JSON schema, with an explicit instruction to estimate rather than omit any mandatory field (full prompt text in Supplementary Material S1).

The calculators $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ invokes are the same modules that generated the reference scores, so $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ceiling is the reference itself and its error is confined to parameter estimation. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ LLM never parses the alternatives; the calculator does, and the default\_params floor condition of the parameter-source ablation (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}) is the control that bounds how much of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s accuracy comes from the calculator alone. Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov} reports what that control buys.

Because the parameters the LLM estimates are scenario-level and identical across the three alternatives, most estimation errors occur in all three raw values together and largely cancel in ranking; ranking accuracy is therefore expected to be robust to weak extraction, whereas the score-level error (MAE/RMSE) and appliance baseline time are the measures that will actually reveal the quality of the extraction.

\subsection{Ablation Designs}
\label{sec:ablation-methods}
Three ablation suites test whether the reported architecture ordering depends on design choices made inside the architectures rather than on the architectures themselves. Each suite varies one design dimension at a time against the shipped configuration, holding criterion weights, temperature, and the scenario set fixed. Perturbations are applied singly rather than factorially, so each suite estimates the effect of one manipulation in isolation and cannot resolve interactions between them.

All three suites share one statistical implementation. Within each stratum a Friedman omnibus is computed per metric, the resulting family of omnibus tests is Holm-corrected, and only the strata whose corrected omnibus survives proceed to post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Cliff's $\delta$ accompanies each surviving pair as an effect size, and percentile bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals are computed per configuration within each stratum independently of the Friedman outcome, since they describe single-configuration uncertainty rather than a comparison. Significance is tested within each model rather than pooled across models, because pooling would confound the effect under test with whichever model happens to be stronger (full test-family bookkeeping in Supplementary Material S2).

All three suites cover the same four models, with Gemini added to each in a final collection pass. Gemini's cells use three runs against the five or ten the other three models use, because its output pricing is roughly fifty times theirs; its run-to-run variance is the lowest of the four models, so three runs resolve its cell means at the precision reported (full variance figures in Supplementary Material S2).

\subsubsection{Prompt ablation}
\label{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}
The prompt ablation asks whether the reported ordering reflects the design of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ or the particular prompts used to implement them. Because that question concerns the shipped comparison, this suite runs on the same 195 Test scenarios that produced it rather than on the 90-scenario RAG corpus. Three perturbations modify one prompt property at a time against the shipped control: removing the anchors that define the endpoints of the scoring scale (no\_anchors), inserting a chain-of-thought scaffold that instructs the model to reason before emitting JSON (cot\_scaffold), and rescaling the response range from 0--1 to 0--10 with scores divided by ten before scoring (scale\_0\_10).

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ ships without anchors, so no\_anchors applies to $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ alone and the matrix has 28 architecture--model--variant cells rather than 32. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is excluded because its LLM call extracts engineering parameters rather than emitting criterion scores, so none of the three manipulations has a counterpart in its prompt. Cells run five times per model, or three for Gemini. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ control and no\_anchors cells were extended to ten runs for DeepSeek, GPT-OSS and Qwen after a five-run pass left the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$/Qwen no\_anchors comparison against control close enough to the significance threshold to warrant more power before drawing a conclusion either way (full run counts in Supplementary Material S2); Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation} reports how it resolved.

The harness re-declares the shipped prompts rather than importing them and asserts at startup that its control condition matches the deployed text, so drift between the ablation and the production prompt fails loudly instead of weakening the baseline in silence. Point estimates use the per-scenario aggregation defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}: each run produces one Kendall's $\tau$ and one Top-1 value per scenario, these are averaged across runs to give one value per variant, architecture, model and scenario, and significance is tested across the 195 scenarios within each architecture--model stratum. Using the main results' aggregation keeps the ablation's point estimates comparable to the main architecture-comparison numbers. The harness separately computes a run-to-run standard deviation per variant, treating runs rather than scenarios as the unit of analysis; that statistic describes how much a single deployment run is expected to vary by chance and is not the quantity tested here.

\subsubsection{RAG ablation}
\label{sec:rag-ablation-methods}
The RAG ablation runs on the complete 90-scenario RAG corpus under a leave-one-out protocol: each scenario serves as the query with itself excluded from retrieval. The corpus supplies both the query set and the retrieval index here because the manipulations under test change the index itself, and the corpus scenarios are the only ones carrying ground-truth scores in both roles.

Seven configurations are compared against the shipped $k=3$ control of the time: retrieval $k$ at 1 and at 5, an embedding swap replacing all-MiniLM-L6-v2 with the smaller paraphrase-MiniLM-L3-v2, exemplars with the hidden engineering parameters removed, exemplar descriptions with the ground-truth scores and ranks removed, random corpus draws in place of similarity-ranked exemplars, and an offline nearest-neighbor configuration that assigns the retrieved scenario's scores directly with no LLM call.

Each configuration answers one question. $k=1$ tests whether a single high-similarity exemplar provides sufficient guidance and $k=5$ whether broader context improves or dilutes the signal. The embedding swap tests how much encoder quality matters. The exemplar-content configurations separate whether the model benefits from seeing the engineering parameters (R-value, SEER, GPM, kWh/cycle) or only the household-reported labels. The score-anchoring configurations test whether the model uses the ground-truth scores as calibration anchors or as noise. Random exemplars control for retrieval quality: if similarity matters, random draws should perform worse than similarity-ranked ones. The nearest-neighbor configuration tests whether unmodified exemplar copying carries ranking signal on its own. On this evidence $k=1$ was adopted as the production retrieval count (Section~\ref{sec:architectures}), since it was not shown to be worse than $k=3$ or $k=5$ in three of the four models, DeepSeek excepted, and carries the lowest token and API cost (full results in Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}).

\subsubsection{Parameter-provenance ablation}
\label{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}
The third suite isolates how much of $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy comes from the LLM's parameter extraction step, as opposed to a deterministic calculator scoring the alternatives on its own (motivation and full results in Section~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}). It also runs on the 195 Test scenarios. All three conditions feed the same reference calculators used to build the ground truth (Section~\ref{sec:gt-calculators}) and differ only in where the withheld engineering parameters come from. true\_params supplies each scenario's true engineering values, so this condition reproduces the ground-truth ranking exactly and is a ceiling by construction (Kendall's $\tau=1.0$, MAE $=0$ for every scenario), not an empirical result about the LLM. extracted supplies the values $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s LLM returned for each scenario, read from the existing per-model result files rather than by re-querying any model. default\_params supplies one fixed value per hidden parameter, the corpus median for numeric parameters and the corpus mode for categorical ones, reused for every scenario with no per-scenario inference at all, making it the floor a calculator-only system would reach if extraction contributed nothing beyond a constant guess. The withheld parameters match those described for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ in Section~\ref{sec:architectures}: R-value, SEER, HVAC age, and occupancy context for HVAC; appliance type, kWh/cycle, and baseline time for Appliance; GPM, tank size, and water-heater setpoint for Shower. The comparison that carries the paper's claim is extracted against default\_params: the gap between them is what extraction contributes over a no-inference baseline, while the gap between extracted and true\_params is the headroom that remains.

Every condition is computed from data already collected for the main benchmark, so this suite makes no new API calls: it re-derives three scorings of the same 195 Test scenarios from files already on disk, and can be rerun after any change to the analysis code at no cost. A scenario whose extraction failed, through a non-JSON response, a missing parameter, or a value outside the calculator's physical validation bounds (Section~\ref{sec:architectures}), is excluded from the extracted condition's metrics for that scenario rather than backfilled with a default, following the sentinel-exclusion convention used throughout this paper (Section~\ref{sec:eval-metrics}). The full results table (Supplementary Material S2) reports the resulting scenario counts per condition in its notes so any exclusion stays visible rather than absorbed into the mean.

\subsection{Alternative ordering}
\label{sec:alternative-order}

Language models weight response options by position as well as by content
\cite{wang2024fairescalers,zheng2023judging}, so a fixed alternative order could introduce bias

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ score one alternative per
API call. Each call names the alternative under evaluation, states the scenario
context, and lists the remaining two alternatives on a single line for context.
The calls are independent and carry no conversation state, so the sequence in
which they are issued cannot affect any individual response. Reversing the
alternative order therefore changes exactly one thing the model can observe:
the order of a two-element list mentioned in passing. Scoring a setpoint of
76\,\textdegree F, for example, the prompt moves from ``Other alternatives
available for this decision: [72, 80]'' to ``[80, 72]'' while the alternative
being scored, the scenario context, and the scoring instructions are
byte-identical. Neither architecture ever presents the three alternatives as a ranked list to choose among, which is the presentation format the position-bias literature concerns. 

In every condition, reversing the order means reversing the three alternative values as they appear in the scenario record, so that the value listed first becomes the value listed last. For $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ this surfaces only in the context line described above. We rescored all 195 test scenarios in reversed order for each of the four models and compared each reversed run against that model's five shipped runs on top-1 correctness. DeepSeek is the only model whose reversed condition was less accurate, by 8.2 scenarios on a net basis; the other three moved by 1.4 to 3.4 in the reversed condition's favour. Because the ranking tie-break resolves exact ties by input order, reversal can flip a fully tied choice set on its own, which biases the comparison toward detecting an effect and makes the observed null conservative. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s shipped runs disagree with each other on top-1 for roughly half of scenarios, so this bounds the effect at about 14 percentage points rather than excluding it. With no material difference between the reversed and shipped conditions, the shipped-order runs were retained as the reported ones (per-model McNemar statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ was included in an initial pass of this test, but its retrieval handles were left uninitialised, so the condition silently measured exemplar removal rather than ordering and was discarded rather than reported.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is not covered by that argument. Its extraction call passes the whole scenario record, including all three alternatives, so reversal changes the order in which the model reads them rather than a list mentioned in passing. Any effect would be indirect, acting through the estimated physical parameters rather than through a score, because the calculator and not the model produces the ranking.

We therefore re-ran $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction over all 195 test scenarios in reversed order, five runs per model, alongside three runs in the shipped order collected in the same session. The contemporaneous control condition is required because the benchmark runs predate the reversed-order condition by several days, and a difference between conditions collected at different times is equally consistent with an ordering effect and with a provider-side model update. In every condition the extracted parameters were passed to the calculator with the alternatives restored to canonical order, so the ranking tie-break cannot manufacture a difference between conditions. Each model therefore contributes 13 runs: five reversed, and eight in the shipped order (five original plus three contemporaneous controls). We test whether the reversed runs are distinguishable from the shipped-order runs by an exact permutation test that reassigns the reversed label across all $\binom{13}{5} = 1287$ ways of choosing five of the 13 runs. Unlike a paired test on disagreements, this uses every scenario in every run, so its floor is $p = 1/1287$.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}
Accuracy is evaluated on scoring and ranking. Scorel error uses Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) between architecture scores and ground-truth scores on the 0--1 MAVT scale, computed separately for each criterion then aggregated across criteria. Ranking accuracy uses Kendall's $\tau$ between the architecture's induced ranking of the three alternatives and the ground-truth ranking, together with Top-1 accuracy (architecture's best-ranked alternative matches ground truth) and Top-2 accuracy (ground-truth top alternative appears in the architecture's top two). Each per-scenario $\tau$ is averaged across the 195 scenarios by arithmetic mean. With $n=3$ alternatives, per-scenario $\tau$ can take only discrete values ($-1, -\frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, 1$) before averaging. 

A uniformly random ranking gives Top-1 accuracy of $1/3$, Top-2 accuracy of $2/3$, and expected Kendall's $\tau$ of $0$. Efficiency is evaluated using total tokens (input plus output) and cost, both summed across the 195-scenario test set.

One alternative failure-handling rule appears in the appendices for comparison. Imputation (Supplementary Material S2) works per run: scenarios that fail receive the 0.5 scale midpoint for the affected criterion scores before that run's metrics are computed, as opposed to dropping the scenario in overall metric calculation.

The unit of analysis differs by test: the paired Wilcoxon comparisons treat the scenario as the unit, computing each of the 195 scenarios' metrics from the mean over the runs in which that scenario was scored successfully, which is five for most scenarios and fewer where a run failed, while the intraclass correlation and the bootstrap confidence intervals operate on per-run aggregate metrics (five runs per architecture--model pair). The paired Wilcoxon tests on the main architecture comparison are Holm-corrected across a single 56-test family (full test-family enumeration in Supplementary Material S2): 55 of the 56 tests remain significant at the 0.05 level, the sole exception being the DeepSeek $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$-versus-$\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ RMSE/MAE-ratio comparison ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 0.093$).

Figure~\ref{fig:grouped_bars} shows the architecture ordering is as expected. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ dominates on every metric for all four models ($\tau \geq 0.88$, Top-1 $> 89\%$), $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ places second ($\tau \sim 0.2$--0.33), and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ ranks third ($\tau$ near zero). Qwen ranks lowest across all three architectures, but the ordering of the other models is not consistent (Gemini leads DeepSeek under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ but trails it under $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$), and the gap between models is smaller for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$. The cost implications discussed in Section~\ref{sec:res-efficiency}.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ being the dominant model is due to the metrics calculation: the LLM estimates scenario-level parameters, while the calculator parses and scores the three alternatives deterministically. Because many extraction errors shift all alternatives together, they largely cancel in the ranking; MAE/RMSE and parameter-specific failure patterns therefore provide the stronger evidence of extraction fidelity. However, the extent to which $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ beats the other architectures, as well as its improvement over a simple fixed input for the calculators (Sections~\ref{sec:res-baselines} and~\ref{sec:res-paramprov}), is notable.

Pairwise differences between architectures are significant under Wilcoxon signed-rank testing within each model taken separately: all 24 tests (two adjacent pairs, three metrics, four models) survive Holm correction, and no cross-model combination is reported because the four models score the same 195 scenarios against the same ground truth, making per-scenario differences positively dependent (full per-test results in Supplementary Material S2).

ICC(1,1), Cliff's~$\delta$, and bootstrap comparisons (Supplementary Material S2) converge on the same conclusion: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage holds consistently across models rather than depending on which model is used. Within every architecture, between-model differences account for most of the metric variance and run-to-run noise for the rest, ranging from 0.55 to 0.83 of the total on Top-1 and Kendall's $\tau$ and above 0.95 on MAE. Which model an architecture runs on therefore matters more than which seed it draws, and the per-model reporting used throughout this paper is the level at which these results are readable.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s scores miss by more than 0.2 points on average and carry almost no ranking information: $\tau = 0.093$ and Top-1 accuracy of 34.1\% sit barely above the 33.3\% chance baseline, and Top-1 accuracy falls below chance in 8 of 12 model--decision-type combinations (6 of 12 by $\tau$). $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ does better on both measures but not reliably: its score error lands between the other two architectures, and its ranking signal, while consistently above chance at 33\%, is too weak to trust for any single decision. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ predictions deviate by only 5--7\% on average and rank correctly in nearly every scenario, picking the best option nine times out of ten at the Top-1 level. 

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ has one weak spot, which is an RMSE/MAE ratio higher than the other two architectures, meaning that large score errors are more common relative to overall error amount.

Interestingly, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$'s failure is not the compression toward the middle of the scale predicted in Section~\ref{sec:lit-review}: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ separates the three alternatives within a scenario more widely than the physics reference does (mean within-scenario range 0.181 for Qwen, 0.210 for GPT-OSS, 0.233 for Gemini, and 0.289 for DeepSeek, against 0.145 for the reference, a factor of 1.25--2.00 with a run-to-run standard deviation of 0.002--0.012, reaching 0.518 against 0.082 on HVAC energy cost), ordering the alternatives incorrectly rather than failing to sufficiently discriminate between them.

Score-level error measures how well $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ reproduces the reference calculator's output, but it does not separate two distinct questions: how accurately the LLM estimates each withheld engineering parameter, and whether an estimation error changes the recommendation. Table~\ref{tab:extraction_accuracy} reports both for the seven numeric parameters. The counterfactual column was then produced by re-running the calculator on each scenario whose extraction of that parameter was wrong, substituting that one erroneous value while holding every other parameter at its true value, and recording how often the top-ranked alternative changed. Reported as a conditional probability, it isolates the consequence of an error in one parameter from the frequency of that error.

Absolute extraction error is substantial. HVAC equipment age is mis-estimated around 6.1--8.5 years on average and shower tank size to within 6.7--8.9 gallons, both large fractions of the plausible range.

Appliance baseline run time is recovered exactly by all four models, and appliance type by three of the four, with Gemini missing 4 of 65 cases (93.8\%). That error rarely propagates to the decision. For most parameters, the conditional probability that an extraction error flips the top-ranked alternative sits below 0.09, and for HVAC equipment age it is zero across all four models. This is because the LLM estimates scenario-level parameters that enter all three alternatives identically, so an error shifts the whole choice set and largely cancels in the ranking. Shower GPM is the consistent exception, carrying the highest flip probability for every model (0.133--0.233), roughly two to three times the next-largest parameter for three of the four models and about 1.5 times for Qwen. GPM is the one extracted parameter that must map a discrete homeowner-facing tier (low\_flow, standard, high\_flow) onto a continuous value that scales both energy and water volume directly, and it is the parameter behind the Shower results being $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s weakest decision type (Section~\ref{sec:res-bytype}). The cancellation that protects the ranking therefore depends on the error being common to all three alternatives, and GPM shows what happens when a parameter is both decision-critical and recovered from a coarse proxy.

\subsection{Isolating the Contribution of Extraction}
\label{sec:res-paramprov}
The error-cancellation mechanism in Section~\ref{sec:res-extraction} is another problem: if extraction errors largely cancel in the ranking, perhaps $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ ranks well simply because it has access to the reference calculator, and the LLM's parameter estimates contribute little. A parameter-source ablation separates the two (design and significance-testing methodology in Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-ablation-methods}). We conducted three conditions on the 195 test scenarios, differing only in where the withheld engineering parameters come from: the scenario's true values, the values the LLM actually returned, and a single corpus-median constant per parameter reused for every scenario. The first condition is the reference ranking itself and so is the ceiling; the third performs no per-scenario inference at all and so isolates what calculator access alone buys. The full per-model, per-condition results table appears in Supplementary Material S2.

Calculator access alone is not sufficient. With no per-scenario inference, the median-parameter condition reaches $\tau = 0.641$ and Top-1 of 76.9\%, well above chance but far below the published $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ results. LLM extraction lifts this to $\tau = 0.897$--$0.921$ and Top-1 of 91.3--94.3\%, a gain of 0.26--0.28 in $\tau$ attributable to extraction rather than to the calculator. 

Friedman/Wilcoxon tests confirm the three conditions differ from one another across the 195 scenarios for every model and metric, and that extraction significantly beats the corpus-median condition on all three metrics for all four models (full statistics in Supplementary Material S2). The remaining gap to the ceiling is negligible on Kendall's $\tau$ and Top-1 for every model and substantial only on MAE, consistent with a residual further score-calibration gap rather than a ranking gap (full statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:res-sensitivity}

To test the stability of the ranking results, both the ground-truth ranking and the architecture rankings were recomputed under a range of alternative weight vectors: an equal-weight vector ($w_j = 0.25$ for all $j$), and the entropy- and MEREC-derived vectors (objective weights computed from how much each criterion's scores actually vary across alternatives, rather than assigned a priori; full derivation in Appendix~\ref{app:weights}), applied pooled and per decision type. Single-criterion $\pm 0.05$ perturbations were also conducted, full results in Supplementary Material S2. They move the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} - \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ gap only within $0.104$--$0.258$ across the four models and never reverse its sign. The full Kendall's $\tau$ matrix by model and weight vector appears in Supplementary Material S2.

$\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ is unconditional: it holds in all 28 model $\times$ weight-vector cells, by a margin of at least $0.305$ Kendall's $\tau$. The $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ advantage over $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is not unconditional. It survives the design, equal and entropy vectors in all four models, but MEREC weights narrow it substantially and in two cells reverse it: $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ overtakes $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ on Kendall's $\tau$ for Gemini under both the MEREC HVAC vector ($0.676$ vs.\ $0.604$) and MEREC pooled ($0.655$ vs.\ $0.616$). Every such case is a MEREC vector, which loads onto Comfort (0.663 for HVAC against the design's 0.200), the one criterion $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ estimates about as well as $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ does (interpretation in Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}; per-model results appear in Supplementary Material S2).

A second check varies the value-function curvature rather than the weights: recomputing the ground-truth scores with the logarithmic shape parameter $\alpha$ swept over $\{1.0,\,1.2,\,1.5,\,2.0\}$ (1.0 is linear) at the baseline weight vector leaves the architecture scores untouched. Figure~\ref{fig:alpha_sweep} shows that for Gemini the ordering $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ is invariant across all four values; the sweep was run on Gemini alone, chosen for its low run-to-run variance (Section~\ref{sec:res-variance}) so that a single run per $\alpha$ is representative without the five-run averaging the other checks in this section use.

\subsection{RAG Ablation}
\label{sec:rag-ablation}
\begin{figure*}[htbp]
	\centering

The RAG ablation (design in Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation-methods}, full values in Supplementary Material S2) yielded three findings. Unlike the corpus-level \texttt{NearestNeighbor} baseline in Section~\ref{sec:res-baselines} ($k=3$ majority vote against the 90-scenario RAG corpus, evaluated on the 195-scenario test set), this configuration retrieves the three most similar scenarios from within the 90-scenario RAG corpus itself under the ablation's leave-one-out protocol and directly assigns the similarity-weighted average of their per-criterion scores, with no LLM call, which gives $\tau = 0.001$ (95\% CI: $[-0.172,\, 0.175]$), indistinguishable from random ranking.

Exemplar content matters in every model; retrieval depth matters in one. Figure~\ref{fig:rag_ablation} shows that removing scores drops $\tau$ substantially for every model, while removing hidden engineering parameters moves $\tau$ far less and in both directions. Only DeepSeek and Gemini carry a Kendall's $\tau$ omnibus that survives the 16-test Friedman family, and within those two the top cluster separates for DeepSeek alone: its $k=1$ configuration scores below the shipped $k=3$ setting at $p_{\text{Holm}} = 0.003$ (Cliff's $\delta = 0.247$, small), whereas Gemini's six top-cluster pairs all sit at $p_{\text{Holm}} \geq 0.35$. Removing exemplar scores or drawing random exemplars also degrades score accuracy in every model, with MAE roughly half again the top-cluster level (full MAE figures in Supplementary Material S2). $k=1$ remains the recommended production setting on cost grounds for three of the four models, whose $k{=}1$ minus $k{=}3$ Kendall's $\tau$ CI contains zero: Gemini $[-0.163,\,0.142]$, GPT-OSS $[-0.128,\,0.228]$, and Qwen $[-0.047,\,0.326]$. DeepSeek is the exception, whose difference of $-0.32$ carries a CI ($[-0.471,\,-0.160]$) that excludes zero; an interval computed over the four models pooled contains zero ($[-0.120,\,0.052]$), so the pooled interval conceals this per-model reversal. $k=1$ is therefore recommended subject to a per-model check that retrieval depth does not degrade that model's ranking.

\subsection{Prompt Sensitivity}
\label{sec:prompt-ablation}

As found by Sclar et al.~\cite{sclar2024promptsensitivity}, prompt style can have a substantial effect on LLM output. To separate the two, we re-ran both architectures under three controlled prompt perturbations across four models: removing the anchors from the scoring instructions (no\_anchors), adding an explicit chain-of-thought scaffold before the JSON response (cot\_scaffold), and rescaling the response range from 0--1 to 0--10 (scale\_0\_10). The full matrix comprises 28 architecture--model--variant cells, 156 runs, and 30{,}420 scenario-level observations. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ has three variants rather than four because its shipped prompt has no anchors for no\_anchors to remove. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ is not included in this ablation: its single extraction call has no scoring anchors, chain-of-thought scaffold, or response scale for the three perturbations to act on (Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}).

The full per-model, per-variant results table appears in Supplementary Material S2: within each model, every $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ configuration remains above every $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ configuration on Kendall's $\tau$, so no prompt perturbation reverses the architecture ordering for a fixed model. The architecture ordering survives every perturbation when architecture and model are held together, though a weaker model running the stronger architecture can still trail a stronger model running the weaker one ($\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ with Qwen falls to $\tau = 0.102$ under scale\_0\_10, below $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ with DeepSeek at $\tau = 0.167$), the same model--architecture interaction visible in the sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}). 

The shipped prompts are not an unlucky draw. Among the pairwise comparisons that reach significance (protocol in Section~\ref{sec:prompt-ablation-methods}), the control wins every comparison it enters for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$; the few significant $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ comparisons instead reflect that architecture operating near the noise floor rather than any perturbation beating the control (full statistics in Supplementary Material S2). As in the RAG ablation (Section~\ref{sec:rag-ablation}), ranking is more stable under perturbation than calibration: the Friedman omnibus on Kendall's $\tau$ is not significant for any $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ model, while the Top-1 omnibus is significant for DeepSeek, so prompt wording shifts which alternative is ranked first more readily than it shifts the relative order of all three.

However, there are two caveats. First, cot\_scaffold both interfered with both reasoning and  parsing: the scaffold moves the JSON payload behind free-form reasoning text, and the harness must fall back to extracting the last balanced object in the response. That variant records the lowest success rate in the matrix (0.961 for $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$/GPT-OSS, against 1.000 for scale\_0\_10), so part of the result is extraction reliability rather than reasoning quality. Second, the three perturbations were applied one at a time rather than factorially, so these results speak to the effect of each manipulation in isolation and not to interactions between them.

models, so the deterministic calculator absorbs the perturbation before it
reaches the ranking (full per-model statistics in Supplementary Material S2).

collected in the later session, with no manipulation distinguishing the latter
two.

\subsubsection{Threats to External Validity}

The scenario corpus was generated by crossing categorical parameters with representative numeric draws. This combinatorial distribution ensures parameter-space coverage but does not reflect real-world joint distributions. For example, in practice, older houses tend to have worse insulation and lower SEER ratings together. The synthetic corpus decouples these correlations, potentially making ranking easier or harder than in household populations. This affects all architectures equally and does not bias the comparison, but it may reduce accuracy during real world use.

Temperature 0.3 and the five-run-then-average protocol were methodological choices. Lower temperatures reduce variance but may narrow the model's output range; higher temperatures increase diversity but risk incoherent outputs. The reported absolute metric values are specific to this operating point, though the architecture ordering appears robust. Temperature was not ablated.

The benchmark runs were collected between 2026-06-28 and 2026-07-21. Those dates are reconstructed after the fact from version-control commit metadata rather than recorded inside the run artifacts, so they bound collection from above rather than dating it exactly. The shipped runs carry no internal timestamp, and the architectures now stamp a UTC \texttt{collected\_utc} field on every run. Within each architecture the four models were collected together, which makes cross-model comparisons at fixed architecture contemporaneous, while cross-architecture comparisons span up to three weeks and cannot rule out a provider-side model update inside that window.

A further limitation concerns the choice sets supplied to the architectures: each scenario presents three specific alternatives that a household might compare, whereas in practice the decision space is continuous and often unstructured. With $n=3$ alternatives there are only $3! = 6$ possible rankings, and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s correlated scenario-level errors are more likely to cancel in a 3-alternative ranking than in a larger choice set. Per-scenario Kendall's $\tau$ can take only the four values $-1$, $-1/3$, $+1/3$ and $+1$, so every per-scenario $\tau$ reported in this paper is a mean over a four-level variable, and the reported $\tau$ of 0.899 is an upper bound on $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s ranking accuracy for larger choice sets. The claim that alternatives near the ones actually tested would produce similar results rests on a monotonicity assumption that holds for cost and energy criteria but may not hold for comfort; extending the framework from ranking accuracy to open-ended alternative generation would be a next step. %todo last sentence too wordy and hard to interpret

The corpus also fixes a narrow population: every scenario sits in one of 48 Pennsylvania municipalities on one of six utility tariffs inside the PJM footprint. It also assumes an occupant free to control the thermostat, appliance run time, and water-heater configuration, so a renter who cannot change the setpoint or replace the heater, or any household outside PJM, is out of scope. The comfort and practicality criteria each encode one account of a reasonable household---ASHRAE\,55 setpoint optima for comfort, and a flat per-hour delay penalty plus an overnight penalty for run times between 22:00 and 07:00 for practicality. A household that deviates for reasons of health, cost, or habit, such as a shift worker who cannot move a wash to daytime hours, is also out of scope.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}
The most immediate follow-up would strengthen this paper's claims: an independent schema review, an engineer with no connection to this project auditing $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s extraction schema and validation bounds, remains desirable to address the circularity concern raised in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

A second tier would test generalization: cross-grid replication under different emissions--cost correlation structures (e.g., ERCOT's real-time pricing or a high-nuclear European grid where marginal emissions approach zero during surplus) would address the PJM specificity noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. A domain-transfer test would apply $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ to a mature calculator outside energy (e.g., actuarial life-expectancy or portfolio Sharpe-ratio estimation). An adversarial stress test would deliberately violate the calculators' structural assumptions to identify where $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$'s advantage degrades.

A third, longer-term tier would extend the framework's scope: a neurosymbolic verification layer checking extracted outputs against known physical bounds \cite{mattas2026neurosymbolic,ye2023satlm}, multi-turn preference elicitation from multiple household members to move beyond the single-decision-maker assumption (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and a randomized controlled trial comparing LLM-MCDA recommendations against unaided decisions over a sustained household deployment.

\section{Conclusion}
%todo: AHAAN COME BACK TO THIS AFTER FIXES ARE MADE SINCE REST OF PAPER HAS PROBLEMS WQHICH WILL AFFEC THIS. ALSO READ APPENDIX AND SUPPLKENMTARY
\label{sec:conclusion}
This study benchmarked three strategies for integrating an LLM into an MCDA workflow for household energy decisions (direct $\mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$, $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}}$ prompting, and $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$, which uses the LLM only to extract withheld engineering parameters and a deterministic physics calculator to score) against a deterministic MAVT reference calculator across HVAC, Appliance, and Shower decisions. $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ most accurately replicates the reference calculator and compresses the model-capability gap (RQ1), and this ordering held across all four models, though the gap between architectures narrowed for weaker models (RQ2). Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the two principal threats that bound this generalization: one researcher designed both the calculator and the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{H}}$ extractor, and the single-grid (Pennsylvania/PJM) specificity of the ground truth.

The contribution is an evaluation framework and an open reference implementation for LLM-fronted MCDA in residential energy, rather than a new model architecture. The framework supplies three deterministic MAVT calculators, a 285-scenario corpus split into 195 test and 90 retrieval cases, and a harness that reports Kendall's $\tau$, Top-1, Top-2, MAE and RMSE per model and per decision type. Most of the evidential weight sits in the robustness apparatus rather than in the headline ordering: seven weight vectors including entropy- and MEREC-derived ones (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}), a sweep of the value-function curvature parameter over four values, three ablation suites separating retrieval depth, prompt wording and parameter source, and an exact permutation test on alternative ordering (Section~\ref{sec:res-order}). All of it is in the public repository, so a reader can rerun the comparison under a different grid, weight vector, or model without rebuilding the ground truth.

Build the deterministic calculator once, and cheaper models match the ranking accuracy of expensive ones, making production deployment economically viable. Whether the architecture generalizes across grids, domains, and household populations (Section~\ref{sec:futurework}) is the question the next study should answer.

Implied weights are derived by constrained least squares on pairwise preferences between alternatives: for each of the 855 within-scenario pairs, the four criterion-score differences are regressed on the rank difference ($w_j \ge 0$, $\sum_j w_j = 1$), so a criterion with zero variance across a scenario's alternatives carries no discriminatory information and receives no fitted weight (Table~\ref{tab:zero_variance}). Practicality is constant across all three alternatives in 44.8\% of HVAC scenarios and Environmental Impact in 42.0\% of Appliance scenarios, so the recovered implied weights are degenerate, placing all weight on Comfort for HVAC (0/0/1/0) and on Practicality for Appliance (0/0/0/1). Shower, the only decision type in which all four criteria vary in essentially every scenario, is the only one that does not produce a single-criterion corner, returning $(0.211,\,0.789,\,0,\,0)$ and splitting weight between Energy Cost and Environmental Impact.

Entropy weights assign 0.371 to the Environmental criterion (a priori: 0.350), 0.163 to Comfort (a priori: 0.200), and 0.198 to Practicality (a priori: 0.150). All three differences are small, and the entropy vectors are themselves applied as sensitivity vectors in Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}, where the architecture ordering is preserved, supporting the Environmental priority. MEREC's per-decision-type weights diverge sharply from the design vector for HVAC and Appliance and converge for Shower. For HVAC, Comfort rises from 0.200 to 0.663 while Environmental falls from 0.350 to 0.128; for Appliance, Environmental falls from 0.350 to 0.044, its smallest weight. The Shower vector (0.248, 0.244, 0.203, 0.305) sits close to the design values (0.300, 0.350, 0.200, 0.150). The direction of divergence matches the realized within-scenario swing (Table in Supplementary Material S2): the criteria that swing within a scenario carry less than the largest nominal weight, because nominal weights were assigned over the global reference range while each scenario's alternatives span a fraction of it. Applied as sensitivity vectors (Section~\ref{sec:res-sensitivity}), these vectors narrow the $\mathcal{A}_{\text{E}} > \mathcal{A}_{\text{D}}$ ordering, reversing it in two model$\times$vector cells. Implied weights collapse to corner solutions for HVAC and Appliance---all weight on Comfort and Practicality respectively---because those are the only criteria that vary across alternatives in scenarios where the remaining criteria are dominated by zero-variance cases. The corner solutions come from how the calculator is built, not from Environmental or Cost being unimportant: when no alternative in a scenario changes the emissions or cost, those criteria have no variance left for a pairwise regression to use. Entropy weights are therefore the more informative of the two objective comparisons here. Implied weights still serve a purpose: their collapse to corner solutions confirms that the calculator's deterministic structure limits how much a data-driven method can discriminate between criteria. Normative, a priori weighting choices cannot be replaced by that kind of optimization alone.
